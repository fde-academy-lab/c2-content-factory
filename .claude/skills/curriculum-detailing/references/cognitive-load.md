# Cognitive load: counting ideas

The most common detailing failure is putting too many sub-topics into a day.
It is an overestimation of the room, and it happens because sub-topics are
counted as headings rather than as things a mind has to hold.

## What counts as one idea

An idea is anything that needs its own unit. A unit is anything that unlocks
one decision the learner could not make before. So the count is the number of
decision sentences of the form *after this, a learner can decide X, which they
could not decide before*, where the sentence completes without an "and".

| Counts as an idea | Does not count |
|---|---|
| A concept that needs its own grounding, example, framework and worked case | A sub-step inside a framework already being taught |
| A framework or formula the learner will apply | A variant of an idea already in the session, taught as a difference |
| A decision with a threshold | A term, on its own. Terms ride on ideas |
| A failure mode that changes what the learner does | A tool command, if the tool is already established |
| A tool being used for the first time (it is a prerequisite AND a load) | A recall of an earlier session, one line long |

If the room has to hold it to use the next thing, it is an idea. If it is
explained and then never used again in the session, it should not be there at
all.

## The caps

| Block | New ideas | New terms | Status |
|---|---|---|---|
| Two hours | Four at most | One per idea | Standing rule, stated by the course owner |
| Three hours | Five at most | One per idea | Construction, derived from the two-hour rule with fatigue allowed for |
| Full day, six hours | Three blocks, one of which is practice with no new ideas, so eight at most | One per idea | Construction, consistent with the session rhythm of one activity every 45 minutes and a build slot |

The two-hour number is the one that has been tested in rooms. The other two
are scaled from it and should be revised the first time a full day is measured
against a transcript.

More than the cap, with jargon on top, produces an overwhelmed room. Fewer
than two ideas in two hours, described rather than worked, produces an
underwhelmed one. Both are failures and the second is harder to see in the
plan, because the timeline was honoured.

## The term rule

One new term per idea, and the term is introduced after the room has seen the
problem it names. A term introduced before its problem is jargon. The same term
introduced after its problem is a label for something the room already has.

Plain self-decoding words come first. The formal term arrives last and is
attached to the thing the room just watched work.

## The basics budget

Starting from scratch and not dwelling on basics are both required. The
mechanism that satisfies both:

- No prior knowledge of the topic is assumed
- The basics are taught through the first application, while taking it apart,
  never as a preamble
- The budget is fifteen minutes in a two-hour block, stated in the session row
- Anyone who needs more than that gets the pre-read before and the weekly
  remedial task after

A session that spends forty minutes on basics has not started from scratch. It
has started slowly, and the room that already knew the basics has left.

## What to do when over the cap

Never shrink the units to fit. Three moves, in order of preference:

1. **Defer.** Move the idea to the session where it is naturally needed. Most
   over-full sessions contain one idea that belongs two sessions later
2. **Demote.** Make it a variant of an idea already present, taught as a
   difference in one slide, and give it its own unit when it returns
3. **Split.** Two sessions, each with its own narrative and its own first
   working thing inside fifteen minutes

## The under-load tell

A sub-topic sits at ten thousand feet when any of these is true:

- No worked example carrying real values
- No gotcha, no failure, nothing that breaks
- No steps written down and numbered
- The transfer question cannot be answered from the material

That sub-topic was covered. It was not taught. Either give it the missing
slots or drop it and say so, because a described topic costs the same minutes
as a taught one and returns nothing.

## What is established, and what is this course's own

The four-idea cap is the course owner's rule from delivered sessions. It is
consistent with the established finding that working memory holds about four
chunks at once (Cowan, 2001, revising the older seven-plus-or-minus-two of
Miller, 1956), but it was not derived from that research and should not be
presented as if it were.

Cognitive load theory itself is established: Sweller (1988) and the work that
followed separate the load intrinsic to the material, the load added by how it
is presented, and the load spent on building understanding. Detailing reduces
the second so the room has capacity for the third.

Two further established effects shape the rising-altitude rules in
`depth-targets.md`: the worked-example effect (Sweller and Cooper, 1985), where
novices learn more from studying a worked solution than from solving, and the
expertise reversal effect (Kalyuga, Ayres, Chandler and Sweller, 2003), where
the scaffolding that helps novices slows and mildly insults experts. Both are
cited here as established. Everything else in this skill is construction.
