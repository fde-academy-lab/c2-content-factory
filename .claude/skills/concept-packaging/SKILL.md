# Concept packaging

Correct content fails constantly because of how it is packaged. This is the
packaging.

## The four-beat rhythm, per concept

Every concept gets these four, in this order, and nothing else.

| Beat | What it does | Length |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Plain definition** | What it is, in one sentence, no jargon | One line |
| **2. Concrete contrast** | A weak version beside a strong version, with real values | Two lines |
| **3. What you do about it** | The action the audience's role actually takes | One line, always present |
| **4. The sticky handle** | An analogy or a paired mental model they can repeat | One line |

Beat 3 is the one most often missing and it is the one that makes the concept
theirs rather than yours. Write it as an instruction, not an observation.

Around fifty to ninety seconds per concept. If a concept needs longer, it is
two concepts.

## Sticky handles that work

**Analogy.** Map the unfamiliar onto something the audience already operates.
Test it by asking where the analogy breaks, and say that out loud too.

- An agent is like a new intern: effective with clear direction, chaotic unsupervised
- Retrieval is like looking something up before answering rather than trusting memory
- A protocol that standardises tool connections is the Bluetooth of that ecosystem

**Paired mental model.** Two contrasting halves of one sentence. These survive
the session better than anything else on the slide.

- Training gives a model memory. Retrieval gives it context.
- A rule fails loudly. A model fails quietly.
- Acceptance criteria ask whether it did the thing. Evaluations ask how often, on what.
- Coverage buys saving. Confidence buys accuracy. They pull against each other.

**Nesting.** When three terms are confused, state the containment and then the
job: A is the umbrella, B is the toolkit inside it, C is one tool in the
toolkit, and your job is picking the right tool for the problem.

## Quiz design

A quiz at the start is the strongest hook available, and it fails when it needs
knowledge the audience has not been given yet.

| Rule | Why |
|---|---|
| Answerable from ordinary work experience, never from the framework | A question needing the taxonomy you have not taught yet produces silence |
| Every option is something they already recognise | Wrong options should be real things, so the reveal teaches four things at once |
| The reveal establishes a concept | The question is a teaching device wearing a question's clothes |
| Counter-intuitive answers are the best ones | Being wrong out loud is what makes the correction stick |
| Three or four questions, revisited later | Say which slide each answer returns at, and close every loop |

Weak: "Which of these needs an agent?" before agents are defined.
Strong: "A new colleague joins Monday. Which of these could you write down as
instructions they would follow perfectly?" Same taxonomy, no jargon, and the
reveal defines the whole thing.

## Content strategy for a slide

- **Cold open on the failure, not the topic.** Start where it goes wrong, then promise the fix with a number and a time
- **Action titles.** The title states the point as a full sentence and the body proves it
- **Picture, then table, then worked example.** A table arriving before its picture is the most common miss
- **One idea per slide,** and the "what you do about it" line proves you have only one
- **Comparison tables that show the shift,** two columns headed what it used to be and what it becomes. This lands harder than a list of new things

## Splitting a multi-day programme

Programmes fail at the seams rather than inside the sessions.

1. **Find the natural decisions,** not the natural topics. Each session should end with the audience able to make one decision they could not make that morning
2. **One question per segment,** and the segment ends when that question is answered. Five to eight segments in a two-hour session
3. **Name segments as questions,** not as machinery. Learners do not read a curriculum, so the segment names carry the interest
4. **Every session hands the next one something specific.** Name the artefact, not the topic
5. **Alternate the mode.** Teach, then demo, then have them do something. Two-hour blocks of one-way delivery fail regardless of content quality
6. **One live demo per session, scripted click by click.** Improvised demos are consistently the most engaging minutes and the least reproducible
7. **One activity every twenty to thirty minutes,** answerable by selection so it works over a call

## Two failures to check for

**A concept with no beat 3.** If you cannot write what the audience does about
it, you are teaching trivia.

**An analogy nobody in the room uses.** Check the analogy against the actual
domain. A food-delivery example for an aviation audience works for warmth and
fails for credibility, so keep the anchor case inside their world and let only
the throwaway analogies come from outside.
