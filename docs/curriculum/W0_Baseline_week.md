# W0 Baseline week

## Mon 28 Sep 2026 · Arrive and set up · Check-in, the welcome, the environment and the self-rating

### Business scenario of the day

WEEK 0 AT A GLANCE. The week measures where each learner stands before any teaching starts, and it seeds the way of working the programme runs on. Claimed on Monday, measured on Tuesday, agreed in a one-to-one by Saturday: every learner leaves with a signed baseline card that puts the self-rating beside the measured score and names two actions. No client-zero material appears this week; Kalpa opens on Week 1 Monday.

MONDAY. Learners arrive on campus and check in at one help desk. The Programme Head welcomes the cohort with the journey map and the session mechanics, and frames the three seats every later week returns to: the business owner who states a problem in business words, the AI engineer who builds the thing that answers it, and the forward deployed engineer who makes it work inside the client's own systems.
Your role this week: say honestly what you know, find out what you can do on paper without an assistant, and agree what you will fix first.

### Thinking we train, before any tool

A programme that ends in interviews starts by separating what a learner claims from what a learner can do. The self-rating is taken before the diagnostic so the two can be laid side by side; the gap between them is the most useful number of the week, and it belongs to the learner.
The three seats give every later technique a place: a technique is worth learning when some business owner needs its answer, some engineer has to build it, and someone has to make it run where the client works.

### Trainer agenda

1. Arrival and check-in at the help desk run by the ground team (through the first half of the day).
2. Welcome by the Programme Head: the journey map across twenty weeks, the kinds of week, how a teaching day, a Saturday and a build week run, and the three seats (90 min).
3. Setup: GitHub account, the course Codespace opened and one cell run, LMS login, and the GitHub Education student application started, since its verification can take several days (60 min).
4. Self-rating form: A to D in Python, SQL, statistics and problem articulation (15 min).

### Learner outcome

UNDERSTANDS: how the twenty weeks run, what a teaching day, a Saturday and a build week look like, and the three seats.
CAN DO: open the course Codespace, run a cell and log in to the LMS.
CAN HANDLE: a setup step that fails, by taking it to the help desk the same day.

## Tue 29 Sep 2026 · The diagnostic · Four papers by topic, marked against keys the same day

### Business scenario of the day

TUESDAY. Four papers, in person, on paper, without an assistant: Python (60 min), SQL (45 min), statistics and data reasoning (30 min) and problem articulation (30 min). Every answer is marked against a key the same day. The diagnostic is ungraded: it sets each learner's baseline and never enters marks.
After the papers, each learner writes a one-page account of a college project: what was built, their own part in it, what broke, and what they would change.
Your role: you sit the papers cold, and you write the project page as if an interviewer will read it, because on Saturday a room will.

### Thinking we train, before any tool

A baseline is only useful if it is comparable, so every paper is objective or bounded: predict the output, fix the broken line, predict the rows a query returns, choose the honest average, and for problem articulation a bounded list, the top five questions to ask and the top three hypotheses to test for one business ask. Bounded answers can be marked against a key in minutes and compared across the room.
The project page asks for 'my part' and 'what broke' because those are the two places where interview answers about projects fall apart.

### Trainer agenda

1. Python paper (60 min).
2. SQL paper (45 min).
3. Statistics and data reasoning paper (30 min).
4. Problem articulation paper (30 min).
5. The college project one-pager (40 min).
6. Setup fixes and free practice while the papers are marked (remaining time).

### Learner outcome

CAN DO: sit four papers without an assistant and finish inside the time.
CAN DEFEND: the project one-pager, since Saturday's talk is built from it.
STATUS: ungraded; the score is a baseline and is shown only to the learner.

### Subtopics (technique in service of the scenario)

• Python: predict the output of short snippets, fix a broken one, write a small function with a loop and a dictionary
• SQL: predict the rows a query returns; write queries that filter, sort and group
• Statistics and data reasoning: mean against median, spread, reading a simple chart, what a small sample can and cannot show
• Problem articulation: for one business ask, the top five questions and the top three hypotheses
• The college project one-pager: what was built, my part, what broke, what I would change

### Trainer notes

## Wed 30 Sep 2026 · Python brush-up + business problem framing · What the warden actually wants

### Business scenario of the day

WEDNESDAY. The Python brush-up runs in two tracks set from Tuesday's scores, a taught track and a practice track. Then the whole cohort meets for the first class on how an engineer listens to a problem.
THE CASE (neutral, outside client zero). The warden of a student hostel says: "Our mess throws away about a fifth of what it cooks. I want an app that predicts how many students will eat." The mess manager adds: "Students skip dinner without telling anyone, and the vendor bills us for what we order, eaten or not."
Your role: you are the engineer in the room, and you may not propose anything for the first twenty minutes.
On the table: what the warden wants to change and how he would know it changed; who decides, who pays and who is affected; what number would move if this were solved; what you must ask before suggesting an app or anything else.

### Thinking we train, before any tool

A business problem arrives with a solution already attached, and the first skill is to set that solution aside. Four questions come before any proposal: what the pain or the decision is, in the owner's words; who decides, who pays and who lives with the result; what number would move if this were solved, and by how much for it to matter; what is known, what is assumed and what must be asked.
The stated problem ('predict how many will eat') and the real one (food is ordered with no signal of who is coming) usually differ, and the cheapest fix may need no model at all: a sign-out by noon may beat a forecast. Every build week drills this move and Week 16 applies it with a client in the room. The four questions are this course's construction; forward deployed interviews test the same skill as the decomposition round.

### Trainer agenda

1. Python brush-up. Taught track: types, lists and dictionaries, loops and accumulators, functions that return, reading a traceback. Practice track: a GitHub practice set. The two tracks run in parallel (180 min).
2. Problem framing class for the whole cohort: the warden's ask, twenty minutes of questions before any proposal, the four questions, the stated problem against the real one, closing on a five-item ungraded Kahoot so the mechanic is not new on Week 1 Monday (60 min).
3. Practice lab, with the one-to-ones running beside it and the diagnostic make-up for anyone absent on Tuesday (120 min).

### Learner outcome

UNDERSTANDS: a business problem arrives with a solution attached, and four questions come before any proposal.
CAN DO: write the stated problem and a candidate real problem for one ask, and list what must be asked before proposing.
CAN HANDLE: the urge to propose in the first minute, and a stakeholder who has already chosen the solution.
CAN DEFEND: any question on the list, by saying which decision its answer would change.

### Subtopics (technique in service of the scenario)

• Python brush-up: types, lists and dictionaries, loops and accumulators, functions that return, reading a traceback
• The stated problem against the real problem
• The four questions before any proposal: the pain, the people, the number, the unknowns
• When the cheapest fix needs no model
• One-to-ones: the baseline card

### Trainer notes

START FROM: Tuesday's scores set the two tracks; the class assumes nothing.
GO AS FAR AS: every learner writes the stated and the real problem for the warden's ask, and ten questions ranked down to five.
STOP BEFORE: any solution design (Thursday), any Kalpa material, and the revenue tree and the investigation ladder, which stay Week 1's reveal.
COMES LATER: Thursday turns framing into options; every build week drills one move; Week 16 runs the full discovery with a client in the room.
ONE-TO-ONES: fifteen minutes each, beside the practice lab; the card shows the learner's own self-rating and score, one gap and two actions, with both signatures.
CUT FIRST: the second round of questions. Never cut the twenty minutes without a proposal.

## Thu 01 Oct 2026 · SQL brush-up + solution thinking · Three options, two ruled out, one thin first version

### Business scenario of the day

THURSDAY. The SQL brush-up runs in the same two tracks, and then the second class.
THE CASE (neutral, outside client zero). The owner of a coaching centre says: "Get me an AI chatbot for admission enquiries. My competitor has one." Yesterday's questions, asked of her, turn up the real problem: enquiries arrive by phone after the front desk closes, nobody calls back, and about a third of callers never hear from the centre.
Your role: in groups of four you bring her three different ways to stop losing enquiries, rule out two with reasons she would accept, and say what the thinnest version of the third looks like by next Monday.
On the table: what the options are, including the ones with no AI in them; what each costs, how long it takes and what can go wrong; which to rule out and why; what the smallest end-to-end version is.

### Thinking we train, before any tool

Solution thinking starts wide and narrows on purpose. Table at least three options that differ in kind, and make one of them use no AI: a missed-call log with a call-back rota, a web form with an automatic reply, a chatbot. Judge each on the same few measures: what it costs to build and run, how long until it works, what breaks and who notices, and how much of the real problem it removes. Rule options out aloud, with the reason the owner would accept. Then build the thinnest version that runs end to end before making any part of it good.
'Why this and why not that' is the sentence interviews ask for, and the option without a model in it is often the one that survives.

### Trainer agenda

1. SQL brush-up. Taught track: SELECT, WHERE, ORDER BY and GROUP BY on a practice database opened from VS Code. Practice track: a SQL practice set. The two tracks run in parallel (180 min).
2. Solution thinking class: groups of four table three options for the coaching centre, rule out two, describe the thin first version, and present in two minutes each (60 min).
3. Practice lab, with the one-to-ones continuing beside it (120 min).

### Learner outcome

UNDERSTANDS: options must differ in kind; ruling out is a skill; the thin end-to-end version comes before the good version.
CAN DO: table three options with one that uses no AI, compare them on cost, time, risk and fit, and state the thin first version.
CAN HANDLE: a client anchored on a competitor's chatbot, and a teammate in love with the hardest option.
CAN DEFEND: 'why this and why not that' in two minutes.

### Subtopics (technique in service of the scenario)

• SQL brush-up: SELECT, WHERE, ORDER BY, GROUP BY on a practice database
• Options that differ in kind, one of them without AI
• The comparison on cost, time, risk and fit
• Ruling out, with a reason the owner accepts
• The thin end-to-end first version
• The two-minute breakdown and the problem card

### Trainer notes

START FROM: yesterday's four questions; today assumes the problem is framed.
GO AS FAR AS: every group presents three options, two rulings-out and one thin version in two minutes.
STOP BEFORE: architecture, tools and cost models with real prices; Week 16 owns those.
COMES LATER: Build 1 asks for framing in a new domain, Build 2 for trade-offs under unequal error costs, Build 3 for the no-LLM option beside the LLM; Week 16 runs design to deployment.
THE PROBLEM CARD: each learner writes up one real problem from a field they know, on one page, with yesterday's four questions answered; it is due Saturday.
CUT FIRST: the last group presentations run as a gallery walk. Never cut the no-AI option.

### Client zero data (TRAINER ONLY)

Neutral practice database for the brush-up. The coaching-centre case is invented for this class, carries no dataset and has nothing planted.

### In-session exercises

GUIDED: one option judged together on the four measures.
UNGUIDED: groups of four: three options, two ruled out, the thin version, presented in two minutes.
MID-SESSION (10 min): five pairs of options; say whether each pair differs in kind or only in size.

### After-class tasks

• PRACTICE: the daily practice set on CodeChef.
• WRITE: the problem card, one page, due Saturday.
