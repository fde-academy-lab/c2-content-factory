## Mon 26 Oct 2026 · Business metric design · The number the growth plan chases, and what stops it being gamed

### Business scenario of the day

Back from Kalpa Health. Meera has accepted the Week 1 finding, parked the acquisition budget, and asked for a growth plan built on frequency. The first fight is over the number. Marketing proposes 'orders per month' as the company's target; Operations proposes 'app sessions'; the head of Retail-Plus wants 'reorders per member', which the app could inflate by auto-reordering.
Meera: "One number the whole company chases, and the numbers that stop us gaming it. If the target moves and the business does not, I want to know before the board does."
Your role: you propose the metric and its guardrails, and each department head will argue for theirs. You need a definition that survives a room of people paid to hit it.
On the table: what a metric is for and what makes one gameable; a north-star against its guardrails; leading against lagging; the exact definition with numerator and denominator that the company will adopt.

### Thinking we train, before any tool

A metric is a decision instrument, and any target bends behaviour toward itself. A north-star names the outcome the plan exists for; guardrails catch the ways of hitting it that hurt the business; leading indicators move before the north-star does. A definition is complete only when it names its numerator, its denominator, its window, and what it deliberately does not reward.
The proxy test: if a department could hit the number while the business got worse, the number is a proxy that has drifted. Every proposed metric on the table today fails that test in some way, and finding how is the exercise.

### Trainer agenda

1. Three proposed targets; the room says how each could be hit while the business got worse (15 min).
2. Metric as decision instrument; the proxy test; gaming and definitions that resist it (40 min).
3. North-star against guardrails; leading against lagging, on Kalpa's tree (50 min).
4. Definitions: numerator, denominator, window, exclusions; computed on the customer table (45 min).
5. Guided then unguided: the metric definition artifact for the growth plan, with guardrails and a gaming note (60 min).
6. Kahoot and close (20 min).

### Learner outcome

UNDERSTANDS: a metric is a decision instrument; a north-star needs guardrails; leading indicators move first; any target bends behaviour.
CAN DO: define a metric with numerator, denominator, window and exclusions, attach guardrails, and write the gaming note.
CAN HANDLE: a metric that rose while the outcome fell, a proxy that drifted, and a department head defending a number they can inflate.
CAN DEFEND: the definition line by line, including what it deliberately does not reward.

### Subtopics (technique in service of the scenario)

• What to measure and why; the proxy test
• North-star and guardrail metrics
• Leading against lagging indicators
• Definitions: numerator, denominator, window, exclusions
• Gaming, and definitions that resist it
• The metric definition artifact

## Tue 27 Oct 2026 · Stakeholder communication · One slide, five minutes, three people paid to disagree; then the first guesstimate

### Business scenario of the day

TUESDAY. Meera has Monday's metric on her desk and wants it adopted, which means it has to survive the people it will be used to judge. "Thursday's leadership meeting gives you five minutes and one slide. Marketing, Operations and the head of Retail-Plus each lose something under your definition, and each will say so. If you cannot hold the room, the company keeps three numbers." In the afternoon Anand sends something smaller and odder. Before he funds a new store, he wants to know roughly how many customers it could sign up to Retail-Plus in its first year, and he wants the answer in fifteen minutes, without a dataset.
Your role: you put the metric on one slide and defend it against three people paid to disagree, and then you size Anand's question aloud from nothing but assumptions.
On the table: what goes on the one slide and what is left off; how an objection is answered without retreating from the evidence or overclaiming it; how a number is estimated when no data exists; how an estimate is checked for sanity.

### Thinking we train, before any tool

A recommendation is communicated when the person who loses something under it can repeat it accurately. The one-slide answer is the Week 1 note made visible, and it leads with the answer (established: answer-first communication, from Barbara Minto's Pyramid Principle): the claim as the title, the evidence as one number with its denominator or one chart, the caveat in plain sight, the action with its cost. Everything else is left off on purpose, and choosing what to leave off is the skill.
Defence has a shape as well: restate the objection so that its owner agrees with the restatement, answer from the evidence, concede what is true in it, and say what would change your mind. A guesstimate is the same discipline without data. A common four-step approach is to clarify the question, map the calculation into factors, calculate from stated assumptions, and validate the answer against something known. Interviewers score the structure and the sanity check, and hardly ever the final number.

### Trainer agenda

1. Meera's terms for Thursday; the room lists what each of the three department heads loses under Monday's metric (15 min).
2. The one-slide answer: the claim as the title, one piece of evidence, the caveat, the action with its cost; three cluttered slides rebuilt (45 min).
3. Defending under challenge: restate, answer from the evidence, concede, say what would change your mind; two rounds in threes, with one learner presenting, one objecting from a role card and one scoring (60 min).
4. What to leave out: the appendix, and the question it is kept for (20 min).
5. The first guesstimate: Anand's sizing question worked aloud together, then a second one in pairs against the clock, with the sanity check required (45 min).
6. The AI-free business case, individual and written: one page on a short Kalpa brief, handed in (35 min).
7. Kahoot and close; Wednesday's pre-read ships tonight (20 min).

### Learner outcome

UNDERSTANDS: a recommendation is communicated when its loser can repeat it; one slide carries claim, evidence, caveat and action; a defence concedes what is true; a guesstimate is scored on its structure and its sanity check.
CAN DO: build the one-slide answer, defend it for five minutes under role-played objection, size an unknown from stated assumptions, and sanity-check the result.
CAN HANDLE: an objection that is partly right, a slide with nine numbers on it, and an estimate that comes out absurd.
CAN DEFEND: Monday's metric in front of the three people who lose under it.

## Wed 28 Oct 2026 · Market basket, lift and cross-sell · Which pairs lift frequency, and what each is worth

### Business scenario of the day

The plan's first lever is frequency, and merchandising thinks it knows how: bundle products so a Retail-Plus member has a reason to come back. Their proposal pairs everything with the store's best-seller, a Rs 199 phone charger, because 'it is in 80 percent of baskets'. The head of merchandising: "Tell me which pairs to promote, and what each promotion is worth in rupees before I spend on it."
Your role: you rank the pairs and cost the recommendation, and merchandising will defend the charger against your numbers.
On the table: which products are actually bought together more than chance predicts; why the most popular item is the worst bundle partner; how a lift table becomes one costed recommendation; what to do with a strong pair that almost nobody buys.

### Thinking we train, before any tool

Support says how often, confidence says how reliably, lift says whether the pair beats chance, and only lift cannot be fooled by popularity. The best-seller has high confidence toward everything because it is in most baskets; its lift is near one, which means the pairing adds nothing. Merchandising's proposal fails on the one measure that matters.
A recommendation is a pair with lift well above one, support high enough to act on, and an expected value in rupees: incremental orders times margin, less the cost of the promotion. Strong pairs with thin support go on a watch list rather than a campaign.

### Trainer agenda

1. Merchandising's proposal; the room says why 'in 80 percent of baskets' might be the wrong reason (10 min).
2. Support, confidence and lift derived by counting on twelve hand-checkable baskets (60 min).
3. The confidence trap: the charger's high confidence and lift near one (35 min).
4. From a lift table to a costed recommendation; thin support and the watch list (45 min).
5. Guided then unguided: basket analysis on the scenario's order items, one costed recommendation (60 min).
6. Kahoot and close (20 min).

### Learner outcome

UNDERSTANDS: support, confidence and lift answer three different questions, and only lift resists popularity; a recommendation carries an expected value.
CAN DO: compute the three measures by hand and in code on the order items, rank pairs by lift with a support floor, and cost one recommendation.
CAN HANDLE: a high-confidence pair with lift below one, a high-lift pair with thin support, and a stakeholder anchored on the best-seller.
CAN DEFEND: which pairs to promote, which to drop, and the arithmetic behind each.

### Subtopics (technique in service of the scenario)

• Support, confidence and lift, derived by counting
• The confidence trap; lift near one
• Basket analysis on the order items
• The support floor and the watch list
• Cross-sell and upsell reasoning; expected value in rupees

### Trainer notes

START FROM: Monday's metric; today finds the lever that moves it.
GO AS FAR AS: everyone ships the basket analysis with a ranked pair list and one costed recommendation.
STOP BEFORE: the apriori algorithm's mechanics, association-rule libraries; counting carries today.

WHAT THE DATA REVEALS: the charger shows confidence 0.82 toward most items and lift 0.97; the strongest real pair sits in Retail-Plus baskets at lift 3.1. Let the room find both.

## Thu 29 Oct 2026 · Cohort and funnel · Why Retail-Plus frequency fell, and where the loss actually happens

### Business scenario of the day

The head of Retail-Plus wants the cause, not just the lever. "Frequency fell in my tier. Is it everyone, or the members who joined during the monsoon sale and never came back? And the app team says the reorder feature has been fixed, but our blended retention number has not moved. Where are we actually losing people?"
The app team sends the event log: visits, searches, add-to-cart, checkout, per member, with signup dates.
Your role: you tell him whether it is a cohort problem or an everyone problem, and which funnel stage owns the loss; the app team will dispute any stage you blame.
On the table: why a blended retention number can hide a collapse; how a cohort is built and read; where in the funnel the loss sits; which stage to fix first and the evidence for it.

### Thinking we train, before any tool

A cohort fixes the entry moment so time effects stop lying; a funnel localises loss to a stage. A blended retention number can stay flat while every cohort worsens, because a growing mix of new members props the blend. That is the retention version of the Simpson reversal from Week 1 Thursday, and the room should say so.
The funnel is read stage by stage with the right denominator at each step; a stage whose drop is a denominator artefact is not a loss. The fix-first decision names the stage, the cohort it hurts most, and the evidence.

### Trainer agenda

1. The blended retention number and the head of Retail-Plus's two questions (10 min).
2. Cohort construction: who entered when, tracked forward, never blended (50 min).
3. The funnel: stages, drop-off per stage, denominators, the stage that owns the loss (45 min).
4. Retention curves: shape, floor, cohort comparison; the blend decomposed (40 min).
5. Guided then unguided: the cohort study and the funnel on the event log, the stage to fix first (60 min).
6. Kahoot and close (20 min).

### Learner outcome

UNDERSTANDS: a cohort fixes the entry moment; a funnel localises loss; a blended average can hide a decline; every funnel stage needs its own denominator.
CAN DO: build a cohort table and a funnel from the event log, read a retention curve's shape and floor, decompose a blend into cohorts.
CAN HANDLE: blended-flat while cohorts decline, a funnel stage whose drop is a denominator artefact, and an app team disputing the stage you blamed.
CAN DEFEND: which stage to fix first and what evidence says so.

### Subtopics (technique in service of the scenario)

• Cohort construction
• Funnel stages, drop-off, denominators
• Retention curves: shape and floor
• Blended numbers against cohort truth
• The fix-first decision and its evidence

### Trainer notes

START FROM: groupby and time fields are solid (Week 2); the new idea is the entry-moment discipline.
GO AS FAR AS: everyone ships the cohort study, the funnel, and one sentence naming the losing stage and cohort.
STOP BEFORE: survival analysis, lifetime value, tests on retention differences (name Week 1 Thursday as where that instinct goes).
COMES LATER: Friday's forecast leans on the cohort table's time axis; Week 5's target is defined on this table.

## Fri 30 Oct 2026 · Forecasting basics · How much the plan delivers, against a baseline that is hard to beat

### Business scenario of the day

The growth plan goes to the board in two weeks and needs a number: revenue for the next two quarters, against the 15 percent plan line, with Diwali inside the window. Finance's forecast is 'last year plus 4 percent'. Marketing's is a curve from a vendor tool that promises 22 percent. Anand: "Two forecasts, eighteen points apart. Which one do I put in front of the board, and how wrong could it be?"
Your role: you pick a forecast and put an error bar on it, and both Finance and Marketing will argue you chose the wrong one.
On the table: what a forecast must beat before it deserves trust; how trend and seasonality show up in two years of monthly revenue; how forecast error is measured honestly; when 'good enough for this decision' is the right answer.

### Thinking we train, before any tool

A forecast earns trust only by beating a naive baseline on a stated error measure. The two honest baselines are last value carried forward and, for seasonal data, the same month last year. Diwali makes the series seasonal, so the seasonal naive is the one to beat, and it beats most vendor curves on two years of data.
Trend and seasonality are read off the series before any model; the error measure is chosen and fixed before comparing; and the answer to Anand names the forecast, its typical error in rupees, and what would make it wrong. Good enough is a claim about the decision, never about the model.

### Trainer agenda

1. Two forecasts eighteen points apart; the room says what would make either one wrong (10 min).
2. Trend and seasonality read off two years of monthly revenue; the Diwali shape (40 min).
3. Baselines: last value, seasonal naive, drift; built by hand (45 min).
4. Error measured honestly: hold out the last six months, MAE in rupees, the vendor curve against the seasonal naive (50 min).
5. Guided then unguided: the forecast for Anand with its error bar and the good-enough call (55 min).
6. Kahoot, close; tomorrow's transfer drill and recap (20 min).

### Learner outcome

UNDERSTANDS: a forecast must beat a naive baseline; seasonal data needs a seasonal baseline; error is measured on held-out months in the business's units; good enough is about the decision.
CAN DO: read trend and seasonality, build the seasonal naive and drift baselines, hold out and score with MAE, and write the forecast note with its error bar.
CAN HANDLE: a vendor curve that loses to the seasonal naive, a stakeholder anchored on 22 percent, and a Diwali month that dominates the error.
CAN DEFEND: the forecast chosen for the board, its typical error, and what would make it wrong.

### Subtopics (technique in service of the scenario)
