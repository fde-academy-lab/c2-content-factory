# W05 question design: what interviewers reward, and how a practice item earns it

Written 28 October 2026 for the Week 5 build (ML core and ME1, 02 to 07 November 2026). INTERNAL research note for the builder; nothing here ships to a learner as written. The exemplar questions use the row's invented numbers (Rs 40 nudge, Rs 1,200 missed buyer, Rs 60,000 false approval, Rs 3,000 wrongful rejection, the 96 to 4 flag, the tree at 100 and 61, thresholds 0.12 and 0.85); the pack's own seeded data replaces them, every number a learner sees is labelled invented, and any item that names a plant or a trainer-only reveal (settlement_status, the visits-and-searches sign flip, the bowed residuals, the Thursday near-tie, the Student segment as the weakest) stays TRAINER until the room has found it (decision `plants-once-found`). The programme's own tags apply throughout: [S] staple asked everywhere, [F] frequent in GCC and product screens, [SV] service-major screen opener, [D] differentiator.

## The five rules the sources agree on

1. **No stimulus, no thinking.** An item with no situation to read tests recall (NBME Rule 2); Huyen's own bank grounds its items in numbers (1 percent positives, 100K comments, 7 days, an 80/10/10 split); Google re:Work wants "a situation that could be realistically encountered on the job".
2. **The decision carries the item.** Ask which metric, which threshold, which model, and what fact would flip the call. Meta scores "Developing and comparing multiple solutions?" and "Can you connect the business context and needs to the ML decisions?"; Aced's strong candidate "justifies each choice against latency, cost, and data constraints".
3. **The best wrong answer is the error the room makes.** Haladyna: "Make all distractors plausible" and "Use typical errors of students to write your distractors"; NBME: distractors "plausible enough to entice test-takers who do not know the correct answer". A near-miss precise about the wrong grain (the right statistic on the wrong denominator) beats a nonsense option, and equal option length with a varied key position closes the test-wise shortcuts.
4. **Score the reasoning, in money.** re:Work interviewers judge the analytical thought process rather than one right answer; Amazon wants "precise metrics and data that quantify your impact" said in "I" statements; Uber's recruiter: "What really gets me excited is when someone speaks to the specifics." A hire-grade answer names the metric, who asked for it and what a wrong number costs.
5. **One question, many hurdles, many roles.** Huyen: "Ask questions with multiple hurdles", "A good interview is a question that turns into a conversation", and "If a piece of knowledge is easy to acquire, it's not worth testing for." The follow-up ladder and the role swap (CFO, product manager, architect, lead ML engineer, implementation engineer, tester) turn a staple into the organisational-maturity test the cohort fails.

## 1. The rubric: what an effective question has that a generic one lacks

| Property | The generic item | The effective rewrite | Rule and source |
|---|---|---|---|
| Anchored in a concrete scenario with numbers | "What is class imbalance and how do you handle it?" (GeeksforGeeks list, 2026) | "Kalpa's repeat flag is 96 to 4 and the classifier scores 96 percent accuracy. How many repeat buyers did it find, and what do you tell Marketing?" | NBME Rule 2: without a vignette the item "will generally be assessing knowledge recall"; Huyen 7.2 and 7.3 carry numbers in every scenario item |
| Forces a trade-off or a decision | "Explain the difference between precision and recall." | "A missed buyer costs Rs 1,200 and a wasted nudge Rs 40. Which metric leads, which way does the threshold move, and what fact would move it back?" | Huyen 7.3: "When should we use RMSE over MAE and vice versa?"; Meta: compares multiple solutions; Aced: justify each choice against cost |
| A plausible wrong answer that reveals one misconception | Options: overfitting, underfitting, a bug, none of the above | Options are four things a real analyst has done: precision over all rows, recall over flagged rows, F1 as the mean of the two, and the key | Haladyna 29 and 30; NBME Rule 4 (homogeneous and plausible); repo distractor discipline (the near-miss) |
| Needs a diagram or a table read | "What is a confusion matrix?" | "Here are the four cells. Read them aloud in Kalpa's words and compute recall." or "These two learning curves: which model carries the variance?" | Huyen 7.3 item 4 (calculate from the matrix) and item 5.3 (a loss-curve figure); NBME chapter 7 on media inside the vignette |
| Asks for the reasoning the interviewer listens for | "Define overfitting." | "Train 100, validation 61. Name it, say what you check next, and say what you tell the team that saw the 100." | re:Work: judge the thought process; Meta: "Are you providing the reasoning behind a particular solution?"; Huyen zen 8: critical thinking over techniques |
| Varies the role | Every item is asked of "a data scientist" | The same threshold asked as CFO (the error bill), PM (what the customer sees), architect (where the value lives and who changes it), lead MLE (how it is re-derived), implementer (how it is configured and logged), tester (which test proves 0.12 is in force) | re:Work: role-relevant questions; Huyen bad question 4: isolate the skill being evaluated; Aced: well-defined prompts grade execution, open prompts grade structuring ambiguity |
| Has more than one hurdle and becomes a conversation | A single-shot "What is regularisation?" | A ladder: "and if the miss cost halves?", "and in Rohan's lending book?", "and when the base rate drifts?" | Huyen zen 5 and 7; bad questions 3 (one single insight), 6 (open question with one expected answer), 7 (easy questions late) |
| Cannot be answered by look-up or by test-wiseness | "Write the formula for F1." | Passes the cover-the-options test; options of equal length and grammar; no absolutes; key position varies across the file | Huyen zen 4; NBME cover-the-options rule and its testwise table (length, absolutes, grammar, word repeats, convergence); Haladyna 20, 24, 28; `scripts/distractor_audit.py` |

## 2. The question types an Indian 0 to 3 year candidate meets

Formats first: Huyen's pipeline runs a phone screen, a coding challenge and four to eight onsite rounds labelled coding, ML theory, ML implementation and behavioural; Meta's ML loop is up to six 45-minute conversations (coding, ML system design, behavioural); Amazon adds a Bar Raiser from outside the team; Capgemini India and HCLTech run an online assessment, a technical interview and an HR interview, with a client interview for some Cognizant roles. Huyen's junior-versus-senior note matters for this cohort: "When you hire junior engineers, you hire for attitude", and systems design goes mostly to senior candidates, so a 0 to 3 year candidate meets the case as a lighter conversation inside the technical round.

| Type | Who asks it, for which role | What the interviewer is checking | The stem's shape | Source |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Screening staples [S] [SV] | Service majors and GCC first rounds for analyst and junior DS; HR-led technical screens | A definition the candidate can apply in one breath without buzzwords; Huyen: "You should understand ML beyond citing buzzwords." | One line that invites a two-line applied answer: "What is leakage, and how do you catch it before it ships?" | Capgemini: technical interview "Focuses on domain-specific knowledge and practical application"; HCLTech: "puzzle, problems and other out-of-the-box questions" |
| Technical depth [F] | Product companies, GCC modelling teams, AI startups; DS and MLE | How, why and when rather than what; a comparison of two choices; a calculation from a table; a diagnosis from a symptom; verification of one's own output | "Your model performs well on the test split but poorly in production. What might have happened?" (Huyen 7.2) | Huyen 7.1 to 7.3; Meta coding round scores communication, problem solving, coding, verification |
| ML case or system design [D] | Product companies and startups for MLE and DS; a lighter case in GCC analyst loops | Problem framing from the decision, data known at prediction time, features with reasons, a baseline before any model, the metric justified against cost, evaluation and monitoring | "Marketing wants a score per customer. Walk me through it from the decision to the monitoring rule." | Meta's five areas (problem navigation, training data, feature engineering, modelling, evaluation and deployment); Huyen: a baseline is "an important step that many candidates forget"; Aced: start with a logistic regression baseline |
| Techno-functional and client-facing [F] [SV] | Service majors, consulting-style GCC roles, client interviews; analyst and techno-functional DS | Translation both ways between the business's words and the model's words; holding the business, product, architecture and engineering views at once | "Explain to the head of risk why his threshold is 0.85 and Marketing's is 0.12, in words he can repeat to his committee." | Uber: "break down what they've done and explain it", "how their work fits into the bigger picture"; Swiggy VP Data Science: work should connect "to fleet satisfaction, customer retention, and even cost" |
| Behavioural with a technical core [S] | Every loop; Amazon and Meta formalise it | A story with a decision, a number, a disagreement handled and what changed; Meta's five signals (resolving conflict, growing continuously, embracing ambiguity, driving results, communicating effectively) | "Tell me about a time a stakeholder wanted a result you could not stand behind." with the technical core (the 0.99 that leaked) | Amazon STAR, "I" statements, 2 to 3 minutes; Meta: "Provide specific examples about what you did and the resulting impact."; Huyen: "did you only do what you were told or did you seize opportunities and take initiative?" |

## 3. Exemplar questions, six per day

Each row: type and role, the question, a two-line model answer, then the best wrong answer with the misconception it reveals. Numbers are invented and labelled so in any learner file.

### Monday 02 Nov: framing, the split and the baseline, linear and logistic regression

| # | Type, role | Question | Model answer | Best wrong answer and its misconception |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Staple [S], business (marketing lead) | Marketing asks for a score per customer for how likely they are to buy again in ninety days. Is this a prediction problem, a rule or a query, and what three things must hold for it to be a prediction problem? | It is a prediction problem because an input is known on the nudge day (the customer table), the target is known later (repeat within ninety days) and the decision changes with the score (whom to nudge). If the decision did not change with the score, a query or a rule would do and would cost less. | "It is a prediction problem because we have the data." The data makes a dataset; the decision makes the problem. |
| 2 | Technical depth [F], senior ML engineer | You split 70/15/15 with a seed, tune on validation, then try three more feature sets against the test set and keep the best. What is the test score now an estimate of? | After selection against it, the test set is a second validation set and its score is optimistic by the amount the selection bought. Rebuild: select on validation, spend the test once, report that one number with its seed. | "Still the generalisation error, since the test rows were never trained on." Leakage needs no training on rows; choosing by the test score leaks the same way. |
| 3 | ML case [D], product manager | The logistic model scores 96 percent accuracy and the majority-class baseline scores 96 percent. Is the model ready, and what would beating the baseline have proved? | Tying the baseline proves the model has learned nothing a say-no-to-everyone rule did not already know; the score is the class prior. Beating it would prove the features carry signal about repeat buying beyond the base rate, which is the only reason to pay for a model. | "96 percent is high, so it is ready." An absolute score has no meaning without its baseline. |
| 4 | Technical depth with a table read [F], implementation engineer | The regression on next-quarter spend prints a recency coefficient of Rs 240 less per day since the last order (invented) and the residual plot bows. What does the coefficient say in Marketing's words, and what does the plot say about the fit? | Each extra day since the last order goes with about Rs 240 less spend next quarter, other features held, which is an association inside this fit; cause needs an experiment. The bowed residuals say a straight line misses a curve, so Rs 240 averages a slope that is steeper at one end. | "Recency causes lower spend, and the residuals look random enough." A coefficient read as a cause, and a pattern in residuals read as noise. |
| 5 | Techno-functional [F], solution architect | Where in the pipeline is the ninety-day repeat flag computed, from which table, as of which date, and what breaks if someone computes it as of today? | From the orders table over the ninety days after each customer's reference date, with every feature computed as of that reference date only. Computing as of today gives a false zero to customers whose window has not closed and lets any feature that moved after the reference date carry the outcome. | "Compute it from the latest table each run so it is fresh." Fresher is better for features at scoring time; the target needs a frozen reference date. |
| 6 | Behavioural with a technical core [D], tester or business | Marketing wants the score by Friday and your model only ties the baseline. What do you say on Thursday, and what do you offer instead? | I show the two numbers side by side and say the model has not earned its keep. I offer the baseline rule (nudge the segment with the highest observed repeat rate) as the Friday ship with its cost per wasted nudge, ask for time to add the Week 2 and Week 4 features, and name the date of the next check. | "Ship it, since it is no worse than the rule and Marketing is waiting." A tie is free. A model that ties costs maintenance and trust for no gain. |

### Tuesday 03 Nov: the confusion matrix, precision, recall and F1, imbalance, cost-based thresholds, the transfer to lending

| # | Type, role | Question | Model answer | Best wrong answer and its misconception |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Staple with a table read [S], analyst screen | Invented matrix: 400 true repeaters, of whom the model flagged 120; 9,600 non-repeaters, of whom it flagged 480. Compute precision and recall and say each in Kalpa's words. | Precision 120 of 600 is 0.20: one nudge in five lands on a repeater. Recall 120 of 400 is 0.30: the model finds three repeaters in ten and misses seven. | 120 of 10,000. Precision taken over all rows instead of over the rows the model flagged. |
| 2 | Technical depth [S], senior ML engineer | Accuracy is 96 percent on a 96 to 4 flag. Before anything else, which single cell of the matrix do you ask for, and why that one? | The true-positive cell, because on a 96 to 4 outcome the model that predicts no for everyone scores 96 and leaves that cell empty. If it is near zero the model has learned the base rate and nothing else. | "Ask for the AUC." A summary curve asked for before the one cell that exposes the degenerate model. |
| 3 | ML case [F], business (finance) | A missed repeat buyer costs about Rs 1,200 of margin and a wasted nudge Rs 40. Which metric leads, which way does the threshold move, and roughly where does it land? | A miss costs thirty times a false alarm, so recall leads and the model should act long before it is 50 percent sure: a nudge pays when the chance of a repeater beats the cost ratio (the 1-in-N test). The threshold falls far below 0.5; the exact value is derived from the day's own scores, and the row's data puts it near 0.12. | "Use F1, since it balances both." Balance is neutral only when the two errors cost the same, and here they do not. |
| 4 | Techno-functional [D], client-facing (head of risk, Kalpa Financial) | Rohan asks whether the same machinery can score loan applicants, where a false approval costs Rs 60,000 and a wrongful rejection Rs 3,000. In words he can repeat to his committee, why does his threshold sit at the opposite end? | Same matrix, opposite cost ratio: a false approval costs twenty times a wrongful rejection, so precision leads and the model approves only when it is very sure, which pushes the threshold high (the row's data puts it near 0.85). The model is the same; the business decides where the line sits, and the line is a cost statement. | "Lending needs a different, more accurate model." The threshold treated as a model property instead of a business decision. |
| 5 | Technical depth [F], tester | The memo sets the nudge threshold at 0.12. Which test proves the deployed scorer uses it, and which number in the daily log would show it has silently reverted to 0.5? | A test that feeds a row scored 0.20 and asserts a nudge, and a row scored 0.08 and asserts none. The log's nudged share per day should sit near the share of validation rows above 0.12; a fall toward the 4 percent base rate flags the revert. | "Check that accuracy is still 96 percent." Accuracy would barely move on the revert and says nothing about the threshold. |
| 6 | Behavioural with a technical core [F], product manager | Marketing wants one number for the dashboard. Which number do you give, and what do you insist sits beside it? | Recall at the chosen threshold, because the Rs 1,200 miss made recall lead, with the nudge count and the cost per wasted nudge beside it so the number cannot be read as free. One number without its cost is how 96 percent fooled the room on Monday. | "Accuracy, since everyone understands it." Familiarity mistaken for meaning. |

### Wednesday 04 Nov: feature engineering, encoding and scaling, leakage, the column audit, feature importance

| # | Type, role | Question | Model answer | Best wrong answer and its misconception |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Staple [S], analyst screen | What is data leakage, and what is the one question that catches it before it ships? | Leakage is any input the model sees in training that would not be known at prediction time, so the offline score borrows from the future. The catch is the prediction-time question asked of every column: on the morning of the nudge, do we know this value for this customer? | "Leakage is when test rows appear in training." Leakage reduced to row overlap, so a column written after the outcome passes unnoticed. |
| 2 | ML case [D], senior ML engineer (TRAINER until found) | A new column lifts validation from 0.71 to 0.99 overnight. Before anyone celebrates, what do you check, in what order, and what score do you expect afterwards? | First the prediction-time question on that column (when it is written and by which system), then its relation to the target over time, then a retrain without it; a jump that size on one column almost always means the column is written after the outcome. I expect the score to fall back near the honest 0.71 and I say so before the run, so the fall reads as a fix. | "Check for overfitting with cross-validation." A leak mistaken for overfitting; cross-validation leaks the same way and keeps the 0.99. |
| 3 | Technical depth [F], implementation engineer | A forty-value city column, a numeric spend column, and two models in the comparison: logistic regression and a tree. Which encoder, which scaling, and which model does not care? | One-hot the city for the logistic model (forty columns is fine; target encoding waits, since it can leak) and standardise spend for it, because its coefficients and any regularisation read the scale. The tree splits on thresholds, so it ignores scaling and can take an ordinal code. | "Label-encode city 1 to 40 for both models." An integer code treated as neutral; the logistic model reads it as an order and a distance. |
| 4 | Techno-functional [F], business and product | The marketing lead sees basket size at the top of the importance chart and wants to grow baskets to grow repeat purchase. What does the chart prove, and what would you need before agreeing? | The chart ranks how much the model leans on a feature when it predicts; it says nothing about whether changing the feature changes the outcome, and a correlated proxy can carry the rank. Before agreeing I would want the Week 4 experiment logic: move basket size for a random group and read repeat rate after. | "Basket size is the strongest driver, so it is the lever." Importance read as causal weight. |
| 5 | Design item [D], solution architect | Recency, frequency, monetary, cohort age and basket signals for 500,000 customers nightly (invented). Two ways: compute them in SQL in the warehouse, or in pandas inside the training notebook. Size each and pick. | SQL in the warehouse: one pass over orders, minutes, one definition shared by training and scoring. pandas in the notebook: the orders table pulled into memory, slower, and two definitions that drift apart. The warehouse wins; a feature the warehouse cannot express is the fact that flips it. | "pandas, since that is where the model is." Features owned by the model code rather than by the data layer that training and scoring both read. |
| 6 | Behavioural with a technical core [D], tester | A colleague wants the 0.99 shipped tonight and the team lead is tired of your objections. What do you do in the next hour? | I run the prediction-time check on the column, retrain without it, and send both scores with the schema note on the column's write time in one message, framed as a saved campaign. I offer the honest model for tonight and a column audit for tomorrow. | "Escalate to the senior analyst first." Hierarchy asked to settle a fact that a one-hour proof settles. |

### Thursday 05 Nov: overfitting, regularisation, cross-validation, trees and ensembles at recognition depth, when the simpler model wins

| # | Type, role | Question | Model answer | Best wrong answer and its misconception |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Staple with a picture [S], analyst screen | Train 100, validation 61 on a depth-unlimited tree. Name it, and sketch the two learning curves that would confirm the name. | Overfitting: the tree has memorised the training rows. The training curve climbs to 100 and stays; the validation curve stalls or falls as depth grows; the gap between them is the variance the model carries. | "Underfitting, since validation is low." A low level read as bias; the gap, and its direction as complexity grows, names the disease. |
| 2 | Technical depth [S], senior ML engineer | Regularisation is called a brake. What does it trade for what, and how would you see the trade in the two curves as you raise the strength? | It trades fit on the training rows (bias up) for stability across draws (variance down) by penalising large coefficients or deep splits. As strength rises the training curve comes down, the validation curve rises and then falls, and the best strength sits where validation peaks with the gap small. | "It improves accuracy." A trade mistaken for a free gain. |
| 3 | Technical depth [F], implementation engineer | Why five-fold cross-validation instead of one split, and what does the spread across folds tell you that the mean does not? | One split's score carries the luck of that draw; five folds average the luck away so the score is about the model. The spread says how much the score depends on which customers landed where, and a wide spread on the 4 percent class says the mean cannot yet be trusted. | "Because it trains on more data." Cross-validation taken as a training trick when it is an estimation trick. |
| 4 | ML case [D], product manager | The tuned tree beats the regularised logistic model by 0.4 points of recall at the chosen threshold. Which ships, and what would change your mind? | The logistic model, because a gap smaller than the fold spread is a tie, and the simpler model costs less to explain to the committee, to run nightly and to trust when it drifts. A gap wider than the fold spread, or an interaction the committee needs that only the tree captures, would change it. | "The tree, since higher recall is the goal." The headline metric treated as the whole decision. |
| 5 | Techno-functional [F], solution architect | Marketing read that a random forest is more accurate. In one minute, what does a forest do to a single tree's problem, and why does that not settle the choice? | A forest grows many trees on resampled rows and features and averages them, which lowers the variance one deep tree carries. It still has to beat the baseline and the simpler model on validation, and four hundred trees cost more to explain to a committee than one equation. | "A forest cannot overfit." Averaging mistaken for immunity. |
| 6 | Behavioural with a technical core [D], business | The room remembers the 100 percent. How do you talk about the 61 without sounding like you are hiding a failure? | I lead with the picture, both curves with the gap labelled, and one sentence: the 100 was the model memorising last quarter's customers and the 61 is what next quarter would have seen. Then the tuned number and the campaign budget it protects. | "Only show the cross-validated score." Trust rebuilt by hiding the 100; naming it is what rebuilds trust. |

### Friday 06 Nov: the committee memo, the error bill, staleness, the transfer paragraph

| # | Type, role | Question | Model answer | Best wrong answer and its misconception |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Behavioural with a technical core [S], business (committee) | Walk me through the model you built this week and why you chose it, in ninety seconds, as the memo's four parts. | Claim: the regularised logistic model at threshold 0.12 for the nudge. Evidence: cross-validated recall against the baseline and the error bill in rupees. Caveat: weakest on the Student segment for the sample-size reason, stale once the repeat base rate moves. Action: the campaign, its budget and the monitoring rule. | A tour of every algorithm tried. The committee heard the method when it wanted the decision and its cost. |
| 2 | ML case with arithmetic [F], business (finance) | Under the chosen threshold the model nudges 1,000 customers (invented), of whom 150 repeat, and it misses 50 repeaters. Compute the error bill at Rs 40 and Rs 1,200, and say which term the CFO will push on. | Wasted nudges 850 times Rs 40 is Rs 34,000; missed repeaters 50 times Rs 1,200 is Rs 60,000; the bill is Rs 94,000 set against the margin the 150 bring. The CFO pushes on the Rs 1,200 per miss, because it is an estimate of margin and the memo must say where it came from. | The 150 counted as a cost. Every flagged row treated as an error; the true positives are the gain the bill is set against. |
| 3 | Technical depth [F], senior ML engineer | Write the staleness rule in one sentence, and name the first number that moves when the model has gone stale. | Retrain or stop when the observed repeat rate among nudged customers falls below validation precision by more than the fold spread for two consecutive weeks. The first number to move is the base rate of the repeat flag itself, because the scores were calibrated to a 96 to 4 world. | "Retrain every month." A calendar offered as a monitoring rule. |
| 4 | Techno-functional [F], client-facing (head of risk) | What changes and what stays when this pipeline moves to Rohan's loan applicants, and which feature would leak there? | The split discipline, the baseline, the matrix and the memo shape stay. The target (default within twelve months, invented), the costs and so the threshold change, and the weakest segment has to be found again. Anything written after the loan decision, such as repayment status or a collections flag, fails the prediction-time question. | "Everything transfers, since the model is the same." A pipeline taken to be its code when it is its target, its costs and its features. |
| 5 | Design item [D], solution architect and implementation engineer | The committee asks how the model runs on Monday morning. Two ways: a notebook a TA runs, or a scheduled job that writes scores to a table with a log. Size each, pick, and say where the threshold lives. | The notebook costs nothing to build and fails the first morning the TA is away, with no record. The job costs about a day to build and leaves a log the staleness rule can read. The job wins, and the threshold lives in one configuration value the memo names, never inside the code. | "The notebook, since the model is a prototype." A shipped model still treated as a prototype. |
| 6 | Behavioural with a technical core [D], tester or product | A committee member says your model is wrong 85 percent of the time it nudges. Is the number right, and what do you say? | It is right as precision at the chosen threshold, and it was chosen on purpose: each nudge costs Rs 40 and each repeater caught is worth about Rs 1,200, so a hit rate near one in seven pays. I say the number, say the bill, and offer the higher threshold that would raise the hit rate together with the repeaters it would lose. | "The model is 96 percent accurate." The flattering metric reached for in defence; owning the chosen error is the defence. |
