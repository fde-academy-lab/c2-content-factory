## 1. Ten principles of an explorable that teaches

1. **One control, and it is the variable the idea turns on.** MLU-Explain's precision and recall page has one instruction, "Try moving the threshold for yourself!", and the whole page follows that slider; its cross-validation page says "Drag the value of k". Bret Victor's car example locks every slider except the turning rate, then sweeps it, because a reader who can change ten things learns nothing about any of them.
2. **The prediction comes before the reveal.** Nicky Case's "Place Your Bets" pattern: "give them the answer, but only after they've wrestled with the question". Hohman, Conlen, Heer and Chau find the same in the NYT "You Draw It" pieces: "Only after readers make their prediction are they shown the actual data." A prediction is a number or a direction the learner commits to, never a quiz with four sentences.
3. **Linked views: the picture and the number move together, from the same control.** MLU's linear regression page: "try changing the weight and bias terms below to see how the MSE and r-squared change", with the residuals drawn in red on the same screen. Distill's momentum article keeps each labelled value beside its plot. A number that changes while the picture stays still is a calculator; a picture that moves without a number is a cartoon.
4. **The default state is wrong on purpose.** MLU's ROC page opens on a threshold it calls "awful" and improves it in steps; Victor starts the car at a guessed 2 degrees and explores from there. The page opens on the mistake the room would make (the flat baseline, the 0.5 threshold, the leaked column, the degree-12 fit), so the first click is a repair the room has argued for.
5. **Countable marks and real numbers with units, and the count beneath every percentage.** PAIR's "Measuring Fairness" draws one silhouette per person so the room can count the sick people the model missed; MLU prints "Recall: 75%" live beside the matrix cells that made it. Hohman calls this "Connecting People and Data": a mark per person keeps a rate honest. Our pages print 3 of 4 before 75 percent, and Rs before any cost.
6. **The error is drawn as a shape before it is named as a formula.** Setosa's OLS page: "the squared errors are represented as squares, and your job is to choose betas ... so that the total area of all the squares" is smallest. MLU draws residuals as red segments; Seeing Theory lets you drag Anscombe's points and watch the line follow. The formula is named last, as the shorthand for what the eye already measured.
7. **Every curve on the page is the trace of a slider the learner has already moved.** MLU builds the ROC curve by trying thresholds one at a time and only then plots all of them; Victor's "ladder of abstraction" overlays the trajectories of every turning rate after the reader has driven one. A precision-recall curve or a learning curve that appears before the slider has been moved is a slide, and the page has nothing to add to it.
8. **Words guide, the control answers, the sandbox comes last.** Case (2014): "Use interactivity only when interactivity works best, otherwise, supplement it with text & images"; Victor: the author should guide readers rather than drop them in a sandbox. Each control sits beside one sentence saying what to watch, and free play is the last section, as in Case's "Sandbox Mode".
9. **Motion only answers a question that was asked first.** The repo's own teaching-styles note names the failure: "beautiful motion without a question in front of it". Distill's momentum piece animates only the trajectory the parameter changes; nothing else on the page moves. Hohman cites the NYT finding that "only a fraction of readers interact with non-static content", so the page must teach from its still state and reward the click with a change, never greet the reader with one.
10. **One file that works on a projector and a phone, offline, with no build step.** Hohman asks "Will an article's code still run a year from now?" and archives each piece; MLU-Explain itself needs npm, Svelte and D3 to build, so it can be linked and never copied. Our page is seeded invented data in a literal array, inline SVG, type the back row can read, no hover-only state, and no smooth scrolling (which times out `html_sweep.py`).

## 2. One design per day

Shared rules: the data is an invented table embedded in the page, labelled invented, drawn by a seeded script at build time so the projector and the phone print the same numbers; no Kalpa plant appears; every percentage prints its count; the trainer's three clicks are the page's whole run order and the sandbox is below them.

### Monday: what does the fit miss, and what does a probability of 0.71 mean?
- **Question.** Why a line beats the mean, what the residuals confess, and why a probability must come from a curve and never a line.
- **Controls.** A slope handle on the line (drag), with a "least squares" button that snaps it; a "line / S-curve" switch on the right panel. The held-out customers need no control: they are drawn hollow and their error reprints with every move.
- **What redraws.** Left panel: 24 invented kirana customers (recency in days on x, next-quarter spend in Rs on y), 16 filled training dots and 8 hollow held-out dots, the line, a residual segment per dot, and three numbers: the sum of squared residuals on training, on held-out, and the baseline's (the mean line). Right panel: the same 24 on a score axis with their repeat flag as dots at 0 and 1, a fitted straight line or the S-curve, and the prediction for one marked customer.
- **Wrong default.** The line opens flat at the mean (the baseline), so the printed error is the number any model must beat; the right panel opens on the straight line, which predicts 1.3 for the marked customer.
- **Three clicks.** (1) Drag the slope; the room says "the line beats the mean, and the gap is what the model earned". (2) Press least squares; the error is smallest and the residuals bow in a curve; the room says "the pattern left in the residuals is what a line cannot hold". (3) Flip to the S-curve; 1.3 becomes 0.71; the room says "a probability stays between 0 and 1, and 0.71 means 71 of 100 customers at this score buy again".
- **Numbers it prints.** Slope in Rs per day of recency, intercept in Rs, the three error sums, the held-out error against the training error, the marked customer's score, the line's prediction and the curve's probability, and "71 of 100".

### Tuesday: where does the threshold sit when a miss costs thirty times a false alarm?
- **Question.** What accuracy hides at 96 to 4, how each matrix cell costs money, and why the same model lands at opposite thresholds in retail and lending.
- **Controls.** The threshold slider (0 to 1, step 0.01); a business switch (retail nudge / lending approval) that swaps the positive class and the two costs.
- **What redraws.** 100 invented customers as a 10 by 10 grid sorted by score, filled when truly positive (4 of 100 for retail, 90 of 100 "repays" for lending), the slider as a cut through the grid; the four matrix cells with their business labels; accuracy, precision and recall as fractions; the error bill at this threshold; and a strip below where the slider has traced the bill, recall and precision against the threshold.
- **Wrong default.** Threshold 0.5 on retail: TP 0, FP 0, FN 4, TN 96, accuracy 96 of 100, recall 0 of 4, precision "no positives predicted". The empty column.
- **Three clicks.** (1) Read the default aloud; the room says "96 percent accurate and it found nobody". (2) Drag down until recall is 4 of 4; the bill falls on the way; the room says "a miss costs Rs 1,200 and a wasted nudge Rs 40, so the threshold drops". (3) Flip to lending; the trace flips and the cheapest point moves to the top; the room says "the expensive error changed sides, so the threshold moved to the other end".
- **Numbers it prints.** The four cells, the three fractions and their percentages, the bill in Rs as (FP × cost) + (FN × cost) with both terms shown, the cheapest threshold the sweep found, and the break-even cost_FP / (cost_FP + cost_FN): 40 / 1,240 = 0.032 for the nudge and 60,000 / 63,000 = 0.952 for the approval.

### Wednesday: what did the model know on the morning of the nudge?
- **Question.** Leakage as a question about time, and why the score fell and the model got better.
- **Controls.** A draggable "prediction time" marker on a timeline; a column picker that adds one engineered feature at a time (orders per month, days since last order, average basket), each stamped with when it is written.
- **What redraws.** A timeline of one invented canteen regular's month (visits, an order, the nudge morning, delivery, payment cleared, the outcome at +90 days); the columns as cards pinned at the moment they are written, green when known at the marker and red after it; a 30-row invented table whose single-column rule accuracy is counted live per column; the kept-column set and its combined rule's score.
- **Wrong default.** The marker starts after delivery, where the junior stood: the payment-cleared flag is green and the combined score reads 30 of 30.
- **Three clicks.** (1) Ask what happens to 30 of 30 when the marker moves to the nudge morning; the room predicts. (2) Drag it there; the flag turns red and drops out; the score falls to about 22 of 30; the room says "the score fell and the model got better, because the flag was written after the answer". (3) Add orders per month; it is green and the score rises a point or two; the room says "a feature earns its place with a reason and a time stamp".
- **Numbers it prints.** Each column's written-at offset in days from the marker (negative means known), its single-column rule accuracy as a count of 30, the count of columns kept and dropped, and the combined score before and after.

### Thursday: memorise or generalise, and what the brake does
- **Question.** What the two curves say, which side of the trade a model sits on, what regularisation buys, and why one split can lie.
- **Controls.** A degree slider (1 to 12) for the polynomial fit; a ridge strength slider (0 to strong); a "reshuffle the split" button that redraws which 10 of the 30 points are held out.
- **What redraws.** 30 invented points from a curved truth with noise, 20 filled training and 10 hollow validation; the fitted curve; two errors, training and validation, in the same units; a strip below that gains one pair of dots at every degree visited, so the U appears only where the slider has been; a five-split panel listing the validation error of each reshuffle and their mean.
- **Wrong default.** Degree 12, strength 0: the curve threads every training point, training error 0.0, validation error large. The tree at 100 and 61, in miniature.
- **Three clicks.** (1) Read the two numbers; the room names it: "memorising". (2) Slide degree down to 2 or 3; validation falls to its floor while training rises a little, the strip draws the U; the room says "the gap is variance, the floor is bias". (3) Back to degree 12, raise the strength; the curve calms at the same degree and validation lands near the degree-3 value; the room says "regularisation is the brake, a second road to the same place". The reshuffle button, pressed five times, shows the validation number jumping by split while the mean holds: "cross-validation averages away split luck".
- **Numbers it prints.** Degree, strength, training and validation error, the gap, the lowest validation error seen and the degree it sat at, the five split errors and their mean.

### Friday: what will being wrong cost, and when do we stop trusting the model?
- **Question.** The error bill in rupees under the chosen threshold, scaled to the campaign, and a staleness rule written as numbers someone can check.
- **Controls.** The threshold slider carried from Tuesday; a trigger-level slider for the staleness rule (the weekly bill ceiling), with the rule's window fixed at two weeks in a row. One input box for the campaign size (default 20,000 members, invented).
- **What redraws.** The bill as two stacked bars (wasted nudges × Rs 40, missed buyers × Rs 1,200) per 100 customers and scaled to the campaign; the recall and precision at the threshold; a 12-week strip of the invented weekly bill, flat until week 8 and climbing after, with the trigger level as a horizontal band and the alarm week marked.
- **Wrong default.** Threshold 0.5 (the bill is all misses and looks small because nothing is sent) and a trigger set above every week, so the alarm never fires and the drift passes unnoticed.
- **Three clicks.** (1) Read the default; the room says "it looks cheap because it never acts; the whole bill is misses". (2) Move to Tuesday's cheapest threshold; the bars split; the room says "the memo claims this threshold because the bill is lowest with both errors counted, Rs N per 100 and Rs M for the campaign". (3) Lower the trigger until the alarm fires at week 9; the room says "the rule names the number, the ceiling, the window and the owner of the check".
- **Numbers it prints.** The two bar components and their sum per 100 and per campaign, recall and precision as fractions, the threshold, the weekly bill for 12 weeks, the trigger level, and the rule as one sentence with its three numbers and the week it fires.

## 3. Anti-patterns that make a page decorative

Measured on the existing Week 4 explorer (`content/W04/D1/demos/C2_W04_D01_week_explorer_STUDENT.html`: 4,485 lines, 21 scenes, 30 range inputs, 15 selects, 0 `<svg>`, 0 `<canvas>`) and on the fetched sources.
- A control that changes only words. Thirty sliders and no drawing surface means every move reprints a sentence or a CSS meter; nothing is seen.
- A reveal button with no prediction tied to a number, and a control locked until a multiple-choice answer is given; the learner cannot return and try again.
- Twenty-one scenes in one file. Nobody on a projector reaches scene nine; one page answers one question.
- Percentages without counts, numbers without units, a slider without a readout, a trap number that is never shown exactly.
- Motion on load, transitions on everything, scroll-driven steps. A trainer cannot hold a scrollytelling state in front of a room, and `html_sweep.py` times out on smooth scrolling.
- Hover-only information, which dies on a projector and on a phone.
- A sandbox with no question in front of it, or a sandbox first; Case puts it last, Victor keeps the author guiding.
- Colour as the only encoding, grey important text, thin type.
- Random data on each load, so the number on the slide and the number on the page disagree.
- A library, a CDN or a build step. MLU-Explain's pages need npm and Svelte; a page that must be built is a page that will not open in a year.
