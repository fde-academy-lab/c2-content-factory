Experiment A · chapter 2 · invented rows

Does Remove Duplicates bring a payment export back to one row per order?

Situation. Three invented orders worth Rs 7,000 together sit on five rows of a payment export: an order of Rs 1,000 on one row, an order of Rs 2,000 paid in two instalments of Rs 1,200 and Rs 800, and an order of Rs 4,000 the gateway posted twice. Every row carries its order's amount, so a pivot's Sum reads Rs 13,000.

Hypothesis. Removing the rows that are identical in every column brings the total back to the orders' Rs 7,000.

Watch for. How many rows go, and where the middle bar lands against the orders' Rs 7,000.

What happened.

Why it matters. An analyst who ran Remove Duplicates believes the export is clean and ships a total still inflated by every instalment order. On Friday's export it moved the two quarters only from Rs 39,40,95,490 to Rs 39,40,57,740, against the warehouse's Rs 19,84,00,000.

Count each order once by its key; removing identical rows leaves every instalment in.

Experiment B · chapter 1 · invented buyers

Does an average of each buyer's revenue per order give the tree's leaf?

Situation. Three invented corporate buyers spent Rs 30,00,000 on 12 orders. One placed a single order of Rs 6,00,000, one placed 3 orders worth Rs 9,00,000, and one placed 8 orders worth Rs 15,00,000. The tree needs revenue per order.

Hypothesis. Averaging the three buyers' own revenue per order gives a leaf that is too high, because the buyer with one large order counts as much as the buyer with eight, so the tree no longer multiplies back to Rs 30,00,000.

Watch for. The outlined bar in the bridge: rupees the averaged leaf adds that the buyers never spent.

What happened.

Why it matters. On Friday's customer table the same averaging reads Business at Rs 11,66,786 an order against Rs 10,45,740 from revenue over orders, and the tree comes back Rs 2.28 crore above what Business sold.

Make every leaf a ratio of the sums, and multiply the tree back before it goes on a page.

Experiment E · chapter 5 · invented orders

Does a lookup add up all of an order's payments?

Situation. Three invented orders are booked at Rs 10,000, Rs 20,000 and Rs 6,000. The Rs 10,000 order was paid in one payment, the Rs 20,000 order in two instalments of Rs 12,000 and Rs 8,000, and the Rs 6,000 order is unpaid, so the payments sit on four rows.

Hypothesis. A lookup on the order id fetches one payment per order, so it reports Rs 8,000 less collected than the customers paid.

Watch for. The outstanding bar, and whether it shrinks to the one unpaid order.

What happened.

Why it matters. On Friday's export the lookup reports Rs 11,83,81,974 collected and Rs 8.00 crore outstanding, 40.3 percent of booked; adding every payment leaves Rs 17,17,180 short, 0.9 percent, and Anand's team would have chased Rs 7.83 crore that customers had already paid.

Where one order meets several payments, add them; the join lives in the warehouse, and a SUMIFS in the sheet only checks it.

11 · Glossary

Which words does the day use, and where did each first matter?

Revenue
Revenue is booked order value in rupees: every order at the price charged, whatever became of it.Chapter 1, the metric at stake.
Revenue tree
The tree splits revenue into three leaves that multiply back to it: customers, times orders per customer, times revenue per order.Chapter 1.
Segment
Kalpa's segments are Retail-Core, the everyday shoppers; Retail-Plus, the paid membership tier; Business, the corporate buyers invoiced in large amounts; and Student.Chapter 1.
Grain
The grain is what one row of a table stands for: a customer in the customer table, a payment in the raw export, an order in the warehouse.Chapter 1, question 1; chapter 2, the trap.
Ratio of sums
A leaf is the pivot's Sum of one field over its Sum of another, so the tree multiplies back; an average of each customer's own ratio gives every customer one vote, and the tree stops multiplying back.Chapter 1, the trap and its fix.
PivotTable
A PivotTable summarises a table by the fields dragged into it and recalculates when someone presses Refresh; a SUMIFS formula recalculates the moment an input changes.Chapter 1, the options.
Quarter
Kalpa's Q1 is April to June 2026 and Q2 is July to September 2026; in Excel the column is =IF(MONTH(date)<=6,"Q1","Q2").Chapter 2, question 1.
First-row flag
=IF(COUNTIF($A$2:A2,A2)=1,1,0) puts 1 on the first row of each order id, so a sum over the flagged rows counts each order once.Chapter 2, the fix.
Remove Duplicates
Excel's Remove Duplicates deletes rows that are identical in every ticked column, so two instalments of one order, which differ in the amount paid, both stay.Chapter 2, the trap.
Control total
A control total is a number someone upstream owns, such as the warehouse's Rs 10,00,00,000 for Q1, and a sheet ties when it reproduces it to the rupee.Chapter 2, the second route; chapter 6, the Checks tab.
Warehouse
The warehouse is Kalpa's Postgres database, one row per order, which Monday's queries read and every number on the deck ties back to.Chapter 2.
Protect list and cut-off
The protect list is the fifty Retail-Plus members with the highest revenue across the two quarters, and its cut-off is the fiftieth member's revenue, Rs 8,580.Chapter 3, question 1.
Exact and approximate match
An exact match finds the id or says it is missing; an approximate match, VLOOKUP's default when its fourth argument is left out, returns the largest id not above the one asked for.Chapter 3, the trap.
XLOOKUP, or IFERROR with INDEX and MATCH
XLOOKUP is exact by default and takes a not-found message as its fourth argument in Excel 2021, 2024 and Microsoft 365; IFERROR around INDEX and MATCH does the same in Excel 2016, Excel 2019 and LibreOffice.Chapter 3, the options.
Period, comparison and base
A front-page number carries the months it covers, what it is compared with, and the base its change is divided by, which is the earlier period.Chapter 4.
Scope and share
The scope is the segments a card covers, printed on the card, and the share is the scope's revenue over the company's in the same quarter.Chapter 4, the trend and the base.
Percentage points
A change in a rate is quoted in points: the consumer segments' share of revenue fell from 0.99 percent in Q1 to 0.83 percent in Q2, 0.16 points, which is a 16 percent fall in the share.Chapter 4, depth.
Booked and collected
Booked is the value of the orders; collected is the money received against them, which needs every payment of every order added.Chapter 5.
Join
A join matches each order with its payments; where one order meets several payments, a lookup takes the first and a sum takes them all.Chapter 5, the trap.
Operating rule
The warehouse owns the number and every join, dedupe and rank Finance relies on; pandas owns the analyst's iteration until Finance relies on it; the workbook owns the last mile on an export that ties, and nobody types over the source.Chapter 5.
Drift check
On every refresh the workbook's control totals, orders and booked revenue per quarter, are compared with the warehouse's, and a mismatch holds the deck until someone knows why.Chapter 5, question 6.
Yellow input
A yellow cell holds an assumption a director may change, and every other cell holds a formula that reads it.Chapter 6.
SUBTOTAL(109) and SUBTOTAL(103)
SUBTOTAL(109) adds only the rows on screen and SUBTOTAL(103) counts them; both leave out rows a filter or a hand has hidden.Chapter 6, the fix.
ISFORMULA
ISFORMULA returns TRUE for a cell that holds a formula, so the Checks tab can find a figure typed over one.Chapter 6, the Checks tab.
Checks tab and release
The Checks tab holds five PASS or HOLD lines, each comparing the sheet with something outside it, and one release sentence that holds the part behind any failing check.Chapter 6.
