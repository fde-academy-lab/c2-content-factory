## Which idea does the chapter 2 set test: what each comparison of two sources can catch?

Two sources agree only when every leaf of the tree agrees as a change, and the way to compare them
depends on what they share. The design items size the comparisons for a file with no shared ids, work
out what each comparison finds when a file does share them and three of its orders differ, and rebuild
two quarters' customers from each customer's own history. The other two items ask which leaves
disagree between two sources with the same revenue change, and what makes a file's customers look
flat.

## Why does each of the five keys hold, from the store file to the app's 153 moving customers?

### Q1. Which comparison fits a store file that shares no ids with the warehouse, sized in the numbers it sets side by side?

Kind: a design item, the best-fit comparison with its size. The store team's file of 90 orders shares
no order or customer numbers with the warehouse, and its revenue change matches the book's.

The key is b, "Twenty numbers: all five leaves for each quarter of each source, compared as Q2 over
Q1". Five leaves (revenue, orders, customers who bought, orders per customer and revenue per order) in
two quarters from two sources make twenty numbers, and reading each as Q2 over Q1 makes a file of 90
orders and a book of 324 comparable. It catches any branch that moved differently, which is the only
disagreement the matching revenue leaves open.

- a, "The two sources' revenue in each quarter, four numbers, since the change already agrees": four
  numbers can only catch a different revenue change, and the stem already says the change agrees.
- c, "Order by order on the till number, 90 lookups against the book's store orders": the till
  numbers match nothing in the warehouse, so all 90 lookups find nothing.
- d, "The store team's spreadsheet rerun on an export of the book's 324 store orders": the platform
  lead ruled out exports, and rerunning the team's own method compares the method with itself.

### Q2. Which comparison finds three changed orders once Finance's file carries the warehouse's order ids?

Kind: a design item, the best-fit comparison with what each one would show, worked out from invented
amounts. Three of Finance's 462 orders differ from the book: Rs 500 more, Rs 300 less and Rs 200
less.

The key is c, "Order by order on the shared id, which names all three while every leaf agrees". The
three differences add to Rs 500 less Rs 300 less Rs 200, which is Rs 0, so Q2's revenue is the same in
both sources and every leaf agrees: the orders and their count are the same, and so are the customers.
Only a match on the shared id compares each order with its twin, and it names all three.

- a, "The two totals, which agree to the rupee, so the file needs no further check": the totals agree
  because the three differences cancel, and three orders still carry a different amount.
- b, "Every leaf as a change, which shows Q2 revenue Rs 1,000 apart and so flags the file": Rs 1,000
  is the three differences added without their signs; the revenues themselves differ by Rs 0, so the
  leaves show nothing.
- d, "Every leaf as a change, which names the three orders through the revenue per order leaf": a leaf
  is one number per quarter and names no order, and here it does not move at all.

### Q3. Which leaves disagree when two invented sources show the same revenue change?

Kind: predict the number, read from the exhibit. Source A goes from 100 orders, 50 customers and
Rs 10,00,000 to 90, 50 and Rs 9,90,000; source B from 400, 160 and Rs 40,00,000 to 360, 144 and
Rs 39,60,000.

The key is a, "Customers and orders per customer, flat in one and down 10 percent in the other". A
keeps 50 customers (0.0 percent) while orders per customer go from 2.0 to 1.8 (down 10 percent); B
loses customers from 160 to 144 (down 10 percent) while orders per customer stay at 2.5. Revenue per
order rises 10 percent in both, from Rs 10,000 to Rs 11,000, and orders fall 10 percent in both, so
0.9 times 1.1 is 0.99 on either side.

- b, "Revenue per order alone, since B's orders are four times A's in each of the quarters": size is
  what the change removes, and revenue per order moves by the same 10 percent in both.
- c, "Orders alone, since B lost 40 orders between the quarters while A lost only 10": reads the
  orders as counts, and as changes both fell 10 percent.
- d, "None, since both revenues fell by the same 1.0 percent from Q1 to Q2 in either source": two
  trees can multiply to the same total with different branches, which is what A and B do.

### Q4. What most likely explains a regional file whose customers held flat while the book's fell?

Kind: spot the plausible wrong output. Every number is invented: the manager's 40 customers all
bought in both quarters, and the book's region went from 100 customers to 88, with 70 in both.

The key is a, "The file was drawn from customers who bought in both quarters, so its customer leaf
could not move". A file built only from customers present in both quarters cannot show anyone leaving
or arriving, so its customer change is zero by construction and every movement lands in orders per
customer instead. The book shows 30 customers who stopped and 18 who arrived.

- b, "The book counts cancelled orders and the file does not, so the book's customers run lower": a
  wider definition would add customers to the book, and the stem gives no sign the two differ on
  status.
- c, "The manager's file is small, and on 40 customers every change is the usual wobble": the flat
  customer leaf is exact by construction, which no wobble explains.
- d, "The book counts a customer who bought in both quarters twice, once in each, so its fall is
  overstated": each quarter counts its own customers once, and a customer in both quarters sits in
  each quarter's count by design.

### Q5. Which rebuild of the app's customers from each customer's history holds, and what does it show?

Kind: a design item, the independent second route. Three filters over one row per app customer gave
their own counts: 52 bought through the app in both quarters, 81 in Q1 only and 72 in Q2 only.

The key is b, "52 plus 81 is 133 and 52 plus 72 is 124, so both quarters tie out and 153 customers
moved". The histories rebuild each quarter from groups no distinct count per quarter produced, so a
wrong count on either side would break a tie-out. They also show what the net fall of 9 hides: 81
customers stopped buying through the app and 72 started, 153 customers moving.

- a, "133 less the net fall of 9 is 124, so 9 customers left the app and every other customer
  stayed": uses the net fall to rebuild itself, so it checks nothing, and 81 customers, not 9, did
  not come back.
- c, "52 plus 72 is 124, so the app kept 52 and added 72, and Q1's 133 cannot be rebuilt": Q2's sum is
  right, and Q1's 133 rebuilds the same way, from the 52 who stayed and the 81 who stopped.
- d, "81 plus 72 plus 52 is 205, so 205 customers bought through the app in Q2": 205 is the app's
  customers over the half-year, and Q2 alone had 124.

## Why is option a in item 2, the totals agreeing to the rupee, worth arguing about?

A total that agrees to the rupee is the result most reviews stop at, and here it is true: Finance's
Q2 revenue and the book's are the same. The argument is about what the agreement proves. Three
differences that cancel leave every total and every leaf where it was, and only the shared id can
name the orders behind them. When two files share ids, the order-by-order match costs 462 lookups and
answers the question Finance will ask next: which orders differ.

## Where did Airbnb's Data Science and Finance teams give two answers to one question?

Airbnb's data team described, on its tech blog in April 2021, how years earlier Data Science and
Finance "would sometimes provide diverging answers using slightly different tables, metric
definitions, and business logic" when the chief executive asked which city had the most bookings in
the previous week. Kalpa's two sources are the same case on a smaller scale: two answers to one
question, settled by setting the definitions side by side and saying which source is the book.
