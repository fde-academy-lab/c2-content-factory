# Which answers hold in the chapter 2 set on whether two sources tell the same story, and why?

Answers: 1b 2c 3d 4a 5b

Last week Meera Raghavan, Kalpa Retail's CEO, parked a Rs 12 crore acquisition budget on a file of 186
orders that said customers held steady while each ordered less often. Chapter 2 set that file beside
the warehouse's book of 1,000 orders: revenue fell 1.6 percent in both, and the customer leaf moved
apart, flat in the file and down 7.0 percent in the book, because every one of the file's 69 customers
bought in both quarters while 170 of the book's 301 did. The set asks the same questions of new
sources. Three of the five items are design items: 1, 2 and 5.

**Who needs the answer.** You, checking your five letters after the lab or tonight. Anand Iyer, the
finance controller, signs only what the book supports, and a comparison that stops at the total is

## Which idea does this set test?

Two sources agree only when every leaf of the tree agrees as a change, and the way to compare them
depends on what they share. The design items size the comparisons for a file with no shared ids,
switch the choice when a file does share them, and rebuild a customer change from each customer's own
history. The other two items ask which leaves disagree between two sources with the same revenue
change, and what makes a file's customers look flat.

## Why is each key right, item by item?

### Q1. Which comparison fits a store file that shares no ids with the warehouse, sized in the numbers it sets side by side?

Kind: a design item, the best-fit comparison with its size. The store team's file of 90 orders shares
no order or customer numbers with the warehouse, and its revenue change matches the book's.

The key is b, "Every leaf in both quarters and both sources, twenty numbers read as changes". Five leaves (revenue, orders, customers who bought, orders per customer and revenue per
order) in two quarters from two sources make twenty numbers, and reading each as a change makes a
file of 90 orders and a book of 324 comparable. It catches any branch that moved differently, which
is the only disagreement the matching revenue leaves open.

- a, "The two sources' revenue in each quarter, four numbers, since the change already agrees": four
  numbers can only catch a different revenue change, and the stem already says the change agrees.
- c, "Order by order on the till number, 90 lookups against the book's store orders": the till
  numbers match nothing in the warehouse, so all 90 lookups find nothing.
- d, "The store team's spreadsheet rerun on an export of the book's 324 store orders": the platform
  lead ruled out exports, and rerunning the team's own method compares the method with itself.

### Q2. Which comparison fits once Finance's file carries the warehouse's own order ids?

Kind: a design item, the fact that switches the choice. Finance's Q2 file carries the warehouse's
order ids and holds 462 orders, the book's count.

The key is c, "Order by order on the shared order id, which names each order that differs". Shared
ids are the fact that makes the order-by-order match possible, and it is then the strongest
comparison: it finds each order that is missing, extra or carries a different amount, which no
comparison of leaves can point to.

- a, "Every leaf as a change, since that comparison caught a moved leaf last week and needs no ids":
  it still works, and it can only say that a leaf moved, where the shared ids can say which order
  moved it.
- b, "The two totals, since the order counts already agree at 462": equal counts can hide an order
  missing on one side and a different one extra on the other.
- d, "None, since two sources with the same count of orders cannot disagree": the same count says
  nothing about which orders, or their amounts.

### Q3. Which leaves disagree when two invented sources show the same revenue change?

Kind: predict the number, read from the exhibit. Source A goes from 100 orders, 50 customers and
Rs 10,00,000 to 90, 50 and Rs 9,90,000; source B from 400, 160 and Rs 40,00,000 to 360, 144 and
Rs 39,60,000.

The key is d, "Customers and orders per customer, flat in one and down 10 percent in the other". A keeps 50 customers (0.0 percent) while orders per customer go from 2.0 to 1.8 (down 10
percent); B loses customers from 160 to 144 (down 10 percent) while orders per customer stay at 2.5.
Revenue per order rises 10 percent in both, from Rs 10,000 to Rs 11,000, and orders fall 10 percent in
both, so 0.9 times 1.1 is 0.99 on either side.

- a, "None, since both revenues fell by the same 1.0 percent from Q1 to Q2 in either source": two
  trees can multiply to the same total with different branches, which is what A and B do.
- b, "Revenue per order alone, since B's orders are four times A's in each of the quarters": size is
  what the change removes, and revenue per order moves by the same 10 percent in both.
- c, "Orders alone, since B lost 40 orders between the quarters while A lost only 10": reads the
  orders as counts, and as changes both fell 10 percent.

### Q4. What most likely explains a regional file whose customers held flat while the book's fell?

Kind: spot the plausible wrong output. Every number is invented: the manager's 40 customers all
bought in both quarters, and the book's region went from 100 customers to 88, with 70 in both.

The key is a, "The file was drawn from customers who bought in both quarters, so its customer leaf
could not move". A file built only from customers present in both quarters cannot show anyone leaving
or arriving, so its customer change is zero by construction and every movement lands in orders per
customer instead. The book shows 30 customers who stopped and 18 who arrived.

- b, "The book counts cancelled orders and the file does not, so the book's customers run lower": a
  wider definition would add customers to the book, and the stem gives no sign the two differ on
  status.
- c, "The manager's file is small, and on 40 customers every change is the usual wobble": the flat
  customer leaf is exact by construction, which no wobble explains.
- d, "The book counts a customer who bought in both quarters twice, once in each, so its fall is
  overstated": each quarter counts its own customers once, and a customer in both quarters sits in
  each quarter's count by design.

### Q5. Which rebuild of the app's Q2 customers from each customer's history holds, and what does it show?

Kind: a design item, the independent second route. On the book, the app's customers who bought fell
from 133 to 124, and 52 bought through the app in both quarters.

The key is b, "133 less 81 who stopped, plus 72 who arrived, is 124, so 153 customers moved". Of Q1's
133, the 52 who came back leave 81 who stopped; of Q2's 124, the 52 leave 72 who arrived. The rebuild
reaches 124 without a distinct count per quarter, and it shows that a net fall of 9 hides 153
customers moving through the app.

- a, "133 less the net fall of 9 is 124, so the app lost 9 customers and kept the rest": uses the net
  fall to rebuild itself, so it checks nothing and misses the 81 who stopped.
- c, "52 who stayed plus 72 who arrived is 124, so the app kept its customers and added some": the
  sum is right and the reading is wrong, since 81 of Q1's customers did not come back.
- d, "133 plus 124 less the 52 in both is 205, so 205 customers bought through the app in Q2": 205 is
  the app's customers over the half-year, and Q2 alone had 124.

## Which wrong answer is worth arguing about?

Item 2, option a. Comparing every leaf is the habit the chapter built, and it still works when the
ids are shared. The argument is about what each comparison can name. A leaf comparison says which
branch moved; a match on shared order ids says which orders moved it, and that is what Finance will
ask for the moment the two sources differ by a single order. The fact that switched the choice is the
shared id, and a good analyst says so in the plan.

## Where does this show up at work?

Airbnb's data team described, on its tech blog in April 2021, how Data Science and Finance "would
sometimes provide diverging answers using slightly different tables, metric definitions, and business
logic" when the CEO asked a simple question about bookings. Every reconciliation between two systems
starts with the same step as this set: read every leaf as a change, then say which source is the book.
