04 · Experiment cards

Four experiments, one change each

Read the situation and the hypothesis, decide what you expect, then run. The values in these cards are invented to isolate one mechanism, and each card says so.

Experiment A · invented counts

Seven orders from four customers

Situation. A shop placed 7 orders from 4 customers last week. The ratio is computed as a count over a count.

Hypothesis. Integer division prints 1 where the honest ratio is 1.75.

Watch for. The lost 0.75, and how many orders the printed ratio accounts for.

What happened. 7 / 4 printed 1. One order per customer accounts for 4 of the 7 orders; the other 3 fell off the end of the division.

Why it matters. A frequency that reads 1 says nobody came back, and a budget moves on a fraction nobody saw.

Divide in numeric, and multiply the ratio back before you trust it.

Experiment B · invented spend

Three members, one who stopped

Situation. Three members spent Rs 100, nothing and Rs 200 this quarter; the one who stopped carries NULL.

Hypothesis. avg reports Rs 150 over two members, and the honest average over three is Rs 100.

Watch for. Which member leaves the denominator.

What happened. With coalesce the average falls from Rs 150 to Rs 100, because the member who stopped is back in the count.

Why it matters. The members a retention team most needs to hear about are the ones avg drops silently.

Put count(*) beside every average, and say whether a NULL means zero.

What happened appears here after the run.

Experiment C · invented rows

Six order rows, four buyers, five members

Situation. A small table of six orders, placed by four of the five members on the book.

Hypothesis. count(*), count(DISTINCT customer_id) and the customers table give 6, 4 and 5.

Watch for. Which count belongs under orders per customer.

What happened. Three honest counts, three questions: rows, people who bought, people Kalpa holds. Orders per customer is 6 over 4, which is 1.50.

Why it matters. A column labelled customers holds whatever the query counted.

Count what you mean: DISTINCT for people, and say which people.

Experiment D · invented table

A reload moves two rows

Situation. Six orders sit in storage order; LIMIT 3 with no ORDER BY returns the first three it reaches.

Hypothesis. Rewriting two rows with identical values moves them to the end, and the same query returns a different three.

Watch for. The ids in the three lit boxes before and after.

What happened. The first three became O-3, O-4 and O-5. No value changed; only where the rows sit did.

Why it matters. An audit sample that cannot be rerun cannot be audited.

Order every list on a unique key, then limit.

05 · Glossary

The day's words

Warehouse
The database every Monday number is computed from, queried in place and never exported.Round 1
Query
A description of the result you want; the database decides how to fetch it.Round 1
count(*) against count(DISTINCT x)
Rows, against different values of x: orders, against customers.Round 1
LIMIT without ORDER BY
Whichever rows the database reaches first, which SQL leaves undefined.Round 1
GROUP BY
One output row per group, with every aggregate computed inside its group.Round 2
WHERE against HAVING
WHERE keeps rows before grouping; HAVING keeps groups after it.Round 2
Integer division
Two integers divide to an integer in Postgres, so 140 / 76 is 1.Round 2
Subquery
A query inside a query, such as the total a share divides by.Round 3
CTE
A named step in a WITH block that later steps can read.Round 3
coalesce
The first value that is not NULL, used to say on purpose that no value means zero.Round 3
