# What should you bring to Tuesday, when the redesign has to survive one slide?

*Illustrative case: the company is real, the situation and the data are synthetic, built for this class.*

**Tuesday's ticket. Uber, Hyderabad, the same case, one day on.** The city head has asked for the
redesign on one slide at Thursday's city review: five minutes, with the driver-operations lead, the
marketing lead and the finance partner in the room. Each loses something under the redesign, and
each will say so. The number the slide must explain, because finance will raise it first: gross
bookings (the fares riders paid) per peak online hour fell from Rs 313.5 in the six weeks before the launch to Rs 260.4 in
the six weeks after, while the average peak fare stayed at Rs 165 and online hours rose 24 percent.
The same rupees read three ways: per online hour, down; per driver-week, counting every row of
`driver_weeks` (6,360 before and 6,500 after), up from Rs 5,816.5 to Rs 5,861.3; per trip, flat at
Rs 165. In the afternoon the city head adds a second ask: roughly how
many drivers must be online at the evening peak on a weekday to keep the median rider wait under
five minutes, answered in fifteen minutes with no dataset and every assumption stated.

Week 4, read on Monday evening: about 15 minutes of reading and one check to run, so that Tuesday
starts on this question from its first minute.

**Who needs the answer.** The city head, who hears the redesign in five minutes at Thursday's city
review before deciding on Friday, and the three people in the room who each lose something under it. A slide that
leads with the wrong number, or hides the caveat they will raise, loses the room before the redesign
is heard.

**The questions on the way.** Which words will Tuesday use that today did not? What should you think
about before you build the slide? What can you check tonight? What line is worth carrying in?

---

## Which words will Tuesday use that today did not?

| Word | What it means on a slide | Monday's example |
|---|---|---|
| Claim | The one sentence the slide exists to say, written as its title | "Pay the floor for completed peak trips, with an acceptance floor" is a claim; "Peak guarantee: six-week review" is only a topic |
| Evidence | The one number that carries the claim, with its denominator said | Trips per peak online hour, 1.90 before and 1.58 after |
| Caveat | What the evidence cannot show, said on the slide where the people who care will see it | The fall could be partly the monsoon's end; the three weeks with most requests already read 1.60 |
| Action | What the reader should do, with its cost in rupees and in weeks | Change the rule for the next six weeks and test it, with the saving stated as a range |
| Appendix | The pages behind the slide, each kept for a question someone will ask | One page per role: the question driver operations, marketing and finance will each raise |

---

## What should you think about before you build the slide?

The same bookings, divided three ways, move three ways: down per online hour, up per
driver-week, flat per trip. Each of the three people in the room will reach for the denominator that
tells their story. Before Tuesday, decide which denominator your slide uses and why, and what you will
say when someone holds up one of the other two.

---

## What can you check tonight?

Work out bookings per online hour for both windows from the ticket's numbers. Gross bookings were
Rs 3,69,93,000 before the launch and Rs 3,80,98,500 after. Divide each by the peak online hours,
118,000 and 146,300. You should reach Rs 313.5 and Rs 260.4. Then multiply the average fare, Rs 165,
by trips per peak online hour, 1.90 before and 230,900 / 146,300 after: the same two numbers come
back, which is why the fall in rupees per hour is the fall in trips per hour wearing a rupee sign.

---

## What line is worth carrying in?

"A slide makes one claim, carries one number with its denominator, shows the caveat where its owner
can see it, and asks for one action with its cost."
