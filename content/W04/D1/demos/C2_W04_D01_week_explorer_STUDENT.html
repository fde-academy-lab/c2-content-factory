Monday, 26 October: is the number we pay for measuring what we want?

Illustrative case: the company is real, the situation and the data are synthetic, built for this class.

Uber, Hyderabad, the driver app. On Monday 31 August 2026 the city team launched a peak-hour earnings guarantee: an enrolled driver, one signed up for the scheme, who stays online through a whole peak window, two a day of three hours each, is guaranteed Rs 300 per peak online hour, and Uber tops up the gap between the driver's trip earnings and that floor.

Six weeks after the launch against the six weeks before, peak online hours rose from 118,000 to 146,300, completed peak trips rose from 224,200 to 230,900, and trips per peak online hour fell from 1.90 to 1.58.

The ask, due Friday: is the scheme working, which metric should it pay for, and is anyone gaming it? The known caveats: the monsoon ended inside the window, 140 drivers joined after the launch, the app counts a driver as online whenever the app is open, and the three outer zones get few requests at any hour.

Who needs the answer. The city head, with the driver-operations lead, the marketing lead and the finance partner in the room.

The questions on the way.

M1

Can online hours rise while riders wait as long?

The first answer most people give: peak online hours are up 24 percent, so supply is up and riders are better served.

Predict before you move anything. As the guarantee rises towards Rs 250 an hour, what will trips per online hour do?

No prediction yet.

Rs 0 an hour

At the case's floor of Rs 300 an hour, online hours rise 24 percent, from 118,000 to 146,300, while trips rise 3 percent, from 224,200 to 230,900. Trips per online hour falls from 1.90 to 1.58, which the city quotes as 1.9 to 1.6, and the median rider wait moved from 4.8 to 4.6 minutes. The fall starts with the first rupees of the floor: on the toy curve, trips per online hour already reads 1.83 at Rs 100.

Online hours count time with the app open, and the driver controls that count. An idle hour in an outer zone earns the same floor as a busy hour in the centre, so the number the scheme pays for rose while riders saw almost no change.

The formula, lasttrips per online hour = completed peak trips / peak online hours = 230,900 / 146,300 = 1.58

M2

Which number should the scheme pay for?

The first answer most people give: pay for whatever went up; online hours rose, so the scheme bought supply.

Predict before you switch. Which choice of paid number makes the most dots drive riders?

No prediction yet.

Paying for online hours

Paying for completed trips with an acceptance floor puts 40 of the 60 dots to driving, against 31 when the scheme paid for online hours. Paying for trips alone lets drivers decline long trips, which the acceptance floor catches, and paying for accepted share lets them accept and then cancel, which paying only for completed trips catches; the definition needs both lines. The deck's fourth choice, enrolled drivers, is not drawn here, since a dot that signs up and never drives looks the same as one that never enrolled: that count rises with every sign-up whether or not anyone drives, which is why the north star counts only drivers online at peak.

A number a driver can raise without serving a rider will be raised that way, so every paid number needs a guardrail that names the way of gaming it.

The formula, last: the definition's linesnorth star, the one number the team steers by: completed peak trips per enrolled driver online at peak, per week
guardrail 1: offers accepted over offers shown, at or above an acceptance floor
guardrail 2: median rider wait at peak
guardrail 3: top-up per completed trip
it does not reward: hours online, offers declined, a shrinking enrolled base

Thursday, 29 October: who is leaving, and at which step?

Illustrative case: the company is real, the situation and the data are synthetic, built for this class.

Zomato Gold, Chennai: the app's paid membership, with free delivery, and its order flow. The membership dashboard reports month-two retention, the share of members two or more months past their signup month who ordered in the month, as flat at 38 percent for June, July, August and September 2026, while every signup cohort's own curve falls with age and each new cohort starts lower: the January 2026 cohort kept 58 percent of its members in month one, the July cohort 49 percent, and the August cohort, grown by a sale week to 13,000 members, 2.6 times a normal month, 31 percent.

On Monday 14 September 2026 a new checkout flow shipped on Android. Since then members' add-to-cart to order on Android fell from 58 to 44 percent, while members on iOS went from 57 to 56 and non-members on Android from 52 to 51. The data hold 53,089 members who joined from January to September, their orders month by month, and the app's events for August and September. The ask, due Friday: is it a cohort problem or an everyone problem, which funnel stage owns the loss, and what should be fixed first. The known caveats: the home carousel opens a menu without a search, sessions and members are different units, and the August cohort was bought on a discount.

Who needs the answer. The membership lead, with the app team, who will say the stage is fine.

Every row falls after month one, and each cohort starts lower than the one before: 58, 56, 55, 53, 52, 50 and 49 percent in month one, then the August cohort's 31. From month two every cohort sits near 38.5 percent and slides about 0.3 points a month. The dashboard reads 38.2, 38.0, 37.9 and 37.8 from June to September, because each month one more cohort of about 5,000 enters its count at about 38.5, just above the older cohorts' slide, and in September the August cohort, 13,000 members in their month one, sits outside it.

In October that cohort enters. If its 31 loses the 10.5 points July's lost, it enters at about 20.5 and the reading falls to about 33: (35,000 x 37.5 + 13,000 x 20.5) / 48,000, where 37.5 is the seven older cohorts' October average after their 0.3-point slide. Any rate from 17 to 24 percent gives 32 to 34, and at a normal 5,000 members the same cohort would pull the reading only to about 35. Counted with the dashed month-one cells too, everyone who joined before the month, September already reads 35.9, so the definition decides whether the dashboard ever looked flat: a blend holds while every part shrinks as long as new parts keep arriving and the weakest part has not been counted yet.

The formula, lastblend = sum of (cohort size x cohort rate) / sum of sizes, over the cohorts the dashboard counts

In January 50 of the 100 older members visited, 50 percent. In February the older members visit 40 of 100 and the January joiners 210 of 400, so 250 of 500 visit: still 50 percent, while the older members fell from 50 to 40.

The blend is an average weighted by size, so a large young group at its high early rate holds it up while every group falls with age: the 400 joiners carry four votes in five. Next month the January joiners age too, and unless another large group joins, the dashboard starts to fall.

Carousel entries are left out

Flipkart, the Lucknow fulfilment centre, festive packer headcount. The centre has 104 weeks of outbound shipments, the weeks beginning 30 September 2024 to 21 September 2026, and an ordinary week in late 2024 ships about 210,000. Diwali fell on 31 October 2024 and 20 October 2025, and falls on 8 November 2026. A packer packs about 120 shipments a shift.

The planner must book packer shifts for the eight festive weeks beginning 12 October to 30 November 2026.
