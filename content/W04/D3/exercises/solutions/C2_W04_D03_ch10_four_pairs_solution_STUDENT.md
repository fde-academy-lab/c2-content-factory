# Which answers hold in the drill on four pairs ranked for action, and why?

Answers: 1c 2a 3b 4a

**Applies:** The support floor and the watch list (chapter 6, items 1, 3 and 4), with lift from chapter
3; The confidence trap; lift near one (chapter 4, item 2).

The drill read four pairs from 40,000 Instamart carts. Under a floor of 1 percent of carts and a lift
of at least 1.5, chips and cold drink (3.39) and onions and tomatoes (1.70) earn slots. Bread and milk
sits at lift 1.04, which is chance. Ice cream and chocolate sauce reads 4.60 on 100 carts, below the
floor, so it goes on the watch list.

**Who needs the answer.** You, to check your four letters. The growth lead defends three slots on
Friday, and each slot given to the wrong pair loses margin on every cart page.

**The questions on the way.**

- Which item is worth arguing about?
- Where does the pattern live at work?

## What does the drill test?

It tests reading lift and support together under a stated rule, the trap of ranking by how often a
pair appears, and two design calls: what to do with a strong thin pair, and why the floor sits where
it does.

## Which item is worth arguing about?

Q4. A floor is a decision, and a different team could defend 0.5 or 2 percent. The argument worth
having is the trade: a lower floor admits more pairs on thinner ground; a higher one admits fewer
pairs that each touch more carts. A floor that moves to fit a favourite pair, as in Q3's option d, is
the one move that cannot be defended.

## Where does the pattern live at work?

Every ranked placement, from a strip to a homepage carousel, needs a rule that says how strong and how
common a pair must be. Writing the rule down before looking at the favourite pair is what keeps the
ranking honest when a stakeholder argues for one item.
