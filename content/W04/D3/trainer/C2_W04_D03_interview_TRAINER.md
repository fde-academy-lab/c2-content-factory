**Where the weak answer scores its two points.** It quotes a number with its unit (0.74 of bread
carts) and names a decision (show milk beside bread), each without the base rate that would have
changed it.

**What a strong interviewer would still push on, and the reply.** "Your floor is 1 percent: why not
0.5?" The floor guards against noise and against rules that fire too rarely to be worth a slot; a
slot worth less would justify a lower floor. "Is 1.04 really chance?" Not chance, cart size: 10,064
shared carts against 9,656 expected is no luck at 40,000 carts (a chi-square near 90), and with
chance counted within each cart size bread and milk read 0.97, below one. Say "below one" in the
room and never write 0.97 on the board, since the lab's plant shares the number. "Could milk ever
earn a slot under your rule?" Not by lift: it cannot pass one over milk's 0.71, 1.41, under the 1.5
bar; only a randomised test in which a milk slot earned as much margin per shopper as the
suggestion it replaced would put it back. "How long does the watch-list trial run?" As long as the
count it needs, fixed before day one: ice cream to sauce reads 0.06, and if every strip add were new
the shown half would buy sauce 3.2 points more often; about 1,100 ice-cream carts an arm detect that
at a 5 percent false alarm and 80 percent power if carts were independent, and about 4,000 detect
half of it. Shoppers are randomised and come back with more carts, so count shoppers, or inflate the
carts by how alike one shopper's carts are. A whole ice-cream cart's margin spreads by about
Rs 28, so reading the gain there would need far more carts, which is why margin is the guardrail and
never the read. The sample-size method itself belongs to the faculty session (tentative).

## What does the [D] transcript say?

Used in the fallback (section 4, 25 minutes): read in pairs, then scored.

**The question as the interviewer says it.** "A category manager is sure the click-through fell
because shoppers buy differently after the monsoon, and wants a two-row redesign that sits higher on
the page, tested next week. You think the ranking caused the fall. What do you say?"

**The clarifying questions a strong candidate asks.** "Which windows do the 6.1 and the 3.8 cover,
and how did each slot's mix of suggestions change between them?" "Did anything else ship on
7 September?" "Is the redesign meant to replace the ranking fix or to run beside it?"

**The structure.** The four moves (restate, answer from the evidence, concede what is true, say what
would change your mind), then one chart and one test design.

**The trap the interviewer fishes for.** Winning the argument: dismissing the manager, or agreeing
to the redesign to keep the peace.

| | Weak answer | Hire-grade answer |
|---|---|---|
| Restate | "That is just a guess." | "You think shoppers changed when the monsoon ended, and that a new layout would win the clicks back." |
| Answer | "The data says the ranking is the problem." | "The mix moved. From 17 August to 6 September milk held 2 percent of impressions, all in slot 3, and from 7 to 27 September it held 50, clicking 0.9 to 1.4 percent across the slots. The other suggestions rose in every slot, 7.5 to 8.2 percent in slot 1, and the four suggestions the strip showed in the same slot in both windows, chips to cold drink among them, each clicked 8 to 12 percent better after the change. Half at 1.2 with half at 6.4 is 3.8. Milk's old cell, slot 3, slipped from 1.2 to 0.9, too little to move the blend. Every day before the switch sat between 5.8 and 6.4 percent, and every day after between 3.6 and 4.0." |
| Concede | Nothing conceded. | "Position matters: among suggestions other than milk, slot 1 draws 8.2 percent against 5.4 for slot 3. Part of that is position and part is that slot 1 holds the strongest suggestion, which a test that shuffles the order separates." |
| What would change it | Not said. | "If the suggestions other than milk had fallen with the blend, the habits story would lead. I cannot prove the monsoon did nothing, and the fall does not need it." |
| Next step, and its worth | "Let us not redesign anything." | "Neither ships blind: ranking (count or lift) and layout (old or two-row) run as a two-by-two test with customers randomised, so each is measured on its own in the same weeks. Taking milk out is worth about Rs 6.3 lakh a month at the central assumptions, Rs 3.9 lakh to Rs 9.3 lakh across them, all gross before the adds that would have come anyway. Every arm is read on margin per customer from items kept at payment, adjusted for each customer's margin in the weeks before, because margin per customer is noisy; the length is fixed before launch from the count that needs, with checkout conversion as the guardrail. The gap between the lift and count arms is the re-rank's worth with those adds netted out." |

**Three follow-ups and the line a hire-grade answer gives each.**

| Follow-up | The line |
|---|---|
| "How do you know the monsoon did nothing?" | "I do not need to: the four suggestions the strip showed in the same slot in both windows each clicked 8 to 12 percent better after the change, so the fall cannot come from shoppers clicking less on the same suggestions. It comes from the mix." |
| "Why not ship both changes together and save a week?" | "Shipped together and blind, neither can be credited; run as a two-by-two with customers randomised, both go live in the same weeks and each gets its own number." |
| "The manager outranks you. Now what?" | "I restate their goal, show the one chart of click-through by slot with milk's share of each slot, and ask for the two-by-two test." |
