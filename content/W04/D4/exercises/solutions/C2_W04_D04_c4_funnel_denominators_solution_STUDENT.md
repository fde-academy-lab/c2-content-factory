# Which answers hold in the drill on funnel stages, drop-off and denominators, and why?

Answers: 1c 2d 3b 4a 5b

The drill read an invented college's admissions. This cycle's steps read 80, 50, 75 and 80 percent,
each over the step before, and multiply to the overall, 24 percent; per applicant the same funnel
reads 26.7, and the chart names its unit. Offer acceptance owns the loss, since it fell from 90 to 75
while shortlisting read 50 in both cycles. The sister programme's 107 percent counts 90 students who
never sat the test, and the test team's question is answered among those who did: 230 of 300, 77
percent.

**Who needs the answer.** You, to check your five letters. The dean sends one admissions team to
whichever step the room says owns the loss.

**The questions on the way.**

- What does the drill test?
- Why is each key right, and each other letter wrong?
- Which item is worth arguing about?
- Where does the pattern live at work?

## What does the drill test?

It tests the four rules of a funnel: each step divides by the step before in one named unit, the
overall divides the last step by the first, a low step is a loss only when it moves against its own
past, and a step above 100 percent is one people can skip.

## Why is each key right, and each other letter wrong?

| Item | Key | Why it holds | Why the others fail |
|---|---|---|---|
| Q1, the step rates | c | 800 of 1,000, 400 of 800, 300 of 400, 240 of 300; overall 240 of 1,000, and 0.80 × 0.50 × 0.75 × 0.80 = 0.24 | a divides every stage by the top, which mixes how far a step is from the start with how well it works; b gives each step's drop-off and the share lost overall; d takes the last step's rate for the overall |
| Q2, the unit | d | 240 of 900 is 26.7 percent; both numbers are true, so the chart says which one it prints | a forgets that the denominator changed; b runs the wrong way, since a person who tries twice can only make the per-person rate higher; c calls a true number wrong, when it answers a different question |
| Q3, the committee | b | Shortlisting read 50 in both cycles because the college selects half by design; the step that moved is acceptance | a blames the lowest step, which did not move; c assumes a funnel's steps lift each other, though each divides by the one before; d blames the test's no-show, which read 20 percent in both cycles |
| Q4, the 107 | a | 320 shortlisted over 300 test-takers puts 90 board-marks students over a step they never passed | b confuses a step rate with a score; c would change both counts, not put the numerator above the denominator; d reads an artefact as a strength |
| Q5, the test team's count | b | Of the 300 who sat the test, 230 were shortlisted | a is the artefact; c and d are true numbers that answer other questions: how much of the field was shortlisted, and how many applications took the test |

## Which item is worth arguing about?

Q3's option d. The test's no-show is the biggest count of lost applications on the page, 200 a
cycle, and a committee that wants the largest number fixed will reach for it. It has read 20 percent
in both cycles, though, so it is how the test runs; fixing it may be worth doing one day, and it
would win back nothing that this cycle lost. SECTION 8 makes that call on the same college with a
third cycle and a cost.

## Where does the pattern live at work?

Every product funnel, from an app's sign-up flow to a checkout, prints step rates, drop-offs and an
overall conversion. The questions a reviewer asks first are the same four: over which step, in which
unit, against which past, and whether any step can be skipped.
