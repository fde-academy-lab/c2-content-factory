# Which answers hold in the drill on retention curves, and why?

Answers: 1c 2a 3d

The drill read the invented gym's three curves. January's floor is 50 percent, first read at age
three, where two ages in a row agree, here two equal ages. Read at age two, the oldest age all three batches have lived,
the order is January 60, February 50 and March 40. March reads 60, 40 and 30 and has not yet shown two
ages within a point, so its floor stays open.

**Who needs the answer.** You, to check your three letters. The gym's owner staffs for the regulars
the floor promises.

**The questions on the way.**

- What does the drill test?
- Why is each key right, and each other letter wrong?
- Which item is worth arguing about?
- Where does the pattern live at work?

## What does the drill test?

It tests the two readings of a retention curve and the rule between them. The shape is how fast a
batch falls; the floor is where it stops. Batches are ranked at the same age, and a floor is called
only when two ages in a row read within a point of each other, which is this programme's own rule; on
a batch of ten that means two equal ages.

## Why is each key right, and each other letter wrong?

| Item | Key | Why it holds | Why the others fail |
|---|---|---|---|
| Q1, January's floor | c | Ages three, four and five all read 50, and the first two equal ages are three and four | a is the start of the curve, the opposite of its floor; b borrows another batch's level, though each batch keeps its own regulars; d is one point on the way down, read before the curve had shown where it stops |
| Q2, the fair order | a | At age two, all three have lived it: 60, 50 and 40 | b ranks by April's calendar column, which caught each batch at a different age; c reads only the size of the first drop, which says nothing about the level each batch keeps; d calls the youngest batch strongest for having had the least time to leave |
| Q3, March's floor | d | 60, 40, 30: no two ages in a row read within a point, since each is 10 or 20 below the one before | a borrows February's floor; b reads a slowing fall as a stop, the same mistake that staffed February for 50; c says the youngest batch never has a floor, though a young batch shows one as soon as it has lived two ages that agree, and March has not yet |

## Which item is worth arguing about?

Q3's option b. A fall that slows from 20 points to 10 looks as if it is about to stop, and often it
is. February looked the same after age two and then fell another 20 points. The two-ages rule is a
rule of thumb this programme uses because it asks the curve to show its floor before anyone plans on
it; a young cohort's open end is what this afternoon's faculty session (tentative) gives a name.

## Where does the pattern live at work?

A product team that plans support staff, inventory or a loyalty budget around its regular customers
reads the floor of each cohort's curve. A floor read from a young cohort's first months is the most
common way that plan comes out too large.
