# Which answers hold in the drill on the fix-first decision and its evidence, and why?

Answers: 1b 2a

The drill asked the dean's two questions on the invented college. Other-state acceptance goes first:
it fell from 87 to 50 percent in the cycle the fee deadline went from 21 to 7 days, while in-state
students held at 92 to 90 on the same step, and the test's no-show has not moved in three cycles. The
call would change if other-state acceptance had already been falling before the deadline changed; it
read 86 and 87. The cost line counts joiners, 48, at an assumed fee of Rs 1 to 1.5 lakh: Rs 48 to 72
lakh of first-year fees.

**Who needs the answer.** You, to check your two letters. The dean signs off one fix, and the finance
office reads the cost line first.

**The questions on the way.**

- What does the drill test?
- Why is each key right, and each other letter wrong?
- Which item is worth arguing about?
- Where does the pattern live at work?

## What does the drill test?

It tests two of the five lines a fix-first page carries: the call, with the fact that would change
it, and the cost, in the unit the business is paid in, with its assumption named and ranged. The
other three, the evidence with its comparator, the objection answered and the split, are SECTION 8's
page.

## Why is each key right, and each other letter wrong?

| Item | Key | Why it holds | Why the others fail |
|---|---|---|---|
| Q1, the order of work | b | The step that moved goes first, and the fact that would change the call is the one that would show the deadline did not cause the fall, which would change the fix | a picks the bigger count, though it never moved, and names a fact that would only confirm the call; c halves the team on a problem that lost nothing this cycle; d waits a cycle, which loses another cycle's students |
| Q2, the cost line | a | Fees come from students who join, 48 of them, and the fee is an assumption, so it carries its range | b counts acceptances, though 2 in 10 of those would not have joined, and gives one figure with no range; c prices the problem that did not move, as if every no-show were a lost joiner, though half of them sitting the test would bring about 30; d has the right unit and drops the range, so one changed assumption breaks it |

## Which item is worth arguing about?

Q2's option d. It counts the right people and uses the middle of the assumed fee, so its Rs 57.6
lakh is a fair central number. A page that prints it alone, though, reads as if the fee were known.
When the finance office asks "what if the fee is Rs 1 lakh?", the range already holds the answer, and
the call survives the question.

## Where does the pattern live at work?

Every prioritisation meeting, from a product team choosing its next sprint to a bank choosing which
branch process to fix, chooses between a big, old problem and a smaller, new one. The page that wins
names the step that moved, its comparator, the cost in the unit the business is paid in with its
range, and the one fact that would change it.
