# Which answers hold in the lab's practice set on an invented tiffin subscription, and why?

Answers: 1c 2a 3b 4a 5d 6b

The set ran the day's method on Dabba Club, an invented tiffin subscription in Pune. January's
cohort kept 6, 4 and 3 of 8 at months one to three. Read at the same age, January is strongest and
March weakest. Menu to specials reads 120 percent because a push notification skips the menu. The
table's lines run age, then active, then the filter, then the pivot. The product lead's 80 percent is
true overall and is answered by splitting payment by renewal, platform and week. A blend that held at
51 percent hid two cohorts falling five points each.

**Who needs the answer.** You, to check your six letters before tomorrow. The same moves return on
Saturday's paper and in every retention question an interviewer asks.

**The questions on the way.**

- What does the set test?
- Why is each key right, and each other letter wrong?
- Which item is worth arguing about?
- Where does the pattern live at work?

## What does the set test?

It climbs the day's ladder on a business nobody has seen: a cohort row by hand, a fair ranking by
age, the skippable step, the order the table's code runs in, the split that answers a partly right
objection, and the headline a flat blend owes its reader.

## Why is each key right, and each other letter wrong?

| Item | Key | Why it holds | Why the others fail |
|---|---|---|---|
| Q1, January's row | c | 6, 4 and 3 of 8, every share over all eight | a divides each month by those active the month before; b drops D07 and D08, who never took a tiffin; d counts those who did not |
| Q2, the fair ranking | a | At the same age, month one: January 6 of 8, February 4 of 6, March 3 of 6 | b compares the cohorts in one calendar month, where the youngest look best; c ranks by size; d waits for a month two that the ranking at month one does not need |
| Q3, the artefact | b | 2,400 sessions reached the specials and only 2,000 opened the menu, because the notification skips the menu | a reads a low step as a loss, though a low step is a loss only when it moves; c is 80 percent, under 100; d cannot be, since a session counts once at a stage |
| Q4, the order | a | Age first, then active, then keep month one on, then the pivot | b runs, and its table carries a month-zero column the joining month fills, the trap that runs without an error; c filters on an age that does not exist yet; d pivots before age exists |
| Q5, the two charts | d | The lead is right overall; the split by renewing against new and by platform shows where payment fell, and the weekly view shows it starting at the update | a shows the overall number the lead already quoted; b says nothing about this app's fall; c shows traffic, never conversion |
| Q6, the headline | b | A: 240 of 400 to 220; B: 270 of 600 to 240; C adds 560 of 1,000; 510 of 1,000 and 1,020 of 2,000 are both 51 percent | a reports the blend that hides two falls; c ranks a cohort in its first month against cohorts in later months; d is false, since a large strong new cohort is exactly what holds a blend |

## Which item is worth arguing about?

Q4's option b. It runs without a single error and prints a table, which is why it is the dangerous
order. Its table gains a month-zero column, filled by the joining month, when nobody can yet have
stopped, and a reader who averages along a row lifts every cohort with it. The habit that catches it
is to read the table's column labels before its numbers.

## Where does the pattern live at work?

A subscription team's monthly review asks these six questions in this order. The analyst who builds
the row by hand first, ranks by age, reads every step over the step before in one unit, and answers
the owning team with the split rather than the total, is the one whose number survives the meeting.
