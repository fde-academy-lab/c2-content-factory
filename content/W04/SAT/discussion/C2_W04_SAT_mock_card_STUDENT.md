# Which interview question will your partner ask, and how is your answer scored?

You work in pairs for 30 minutes. Each of you asks the other one question from this card and one of
the follow-ups listed under it, scores the answer on the scorecard, and gives one sentence of
feedback; then you swap seats. The questions are the week's interview transcripts and their
AI-company variants. Every situation and number in them is the week's illustrative case, with
synthetic data, or invented for the question, as each transcript says, so a question that names a
company states no fact about it. Every hire-grade answer is in that day's transcript, in the day's
`extras` folder, for reading after the round.

**Who needs the answer.** You, before a screen that asks you to say the week's methods aloud, and
your partner, who scores what you say.

**The questions on the way.**

- How does the round run?
- Which questions can you be asked?
- How is an answer scored?

---

## How does the round run?

| Part | Duration | What you do |
|---|---|---|
| Pair up | 2 min | Sit with the partner the Academic TA names, someone who sat apart from you in the paper and at another drill table. |
| Round one | 12 min | The interviewer picks one question and one of its follow-ups, asks the question and waits, then asks the follow-up. The candidate answers aloud with no paper. The interviewer scores the seven lines below and says one sentence: the line that scored lowest and what a 2 would have said. |
| Round two | 12 min | Swap seats. The new interviewer picks a question from a different day, and at least one of the pair's two questions is an AI-company variant. |
| Back to the room | 4 min | Two pairs say which line scored lowest; the Academic TA asks that question to a third person, cold. |

## Which questions can you be asked?

The tag beside each question is the row's: [S] is a staple asked everywhere, and [D] a
differentiator. The AI-company variants are written for this programme and carry no tag. Where a
variant lists no follow-up of its own, ask "What would change your mind?"

### Monday, the number a scheme pays for

| Question, as the interviewer says it | Follow-ups to choose from |
|---|---|
| [S] "Uber launched a peak-hour earnings guarantee for drivers in one city six weeks ago. How would you measure whether it is a success?" | "Online hours rose 24 percent; what do you check before you celebrate?" · "The measured drivers control when they log in; what happens to your number?" |
| [D] "Driver operations wants online hours as the scheme's target. Marketing wants enrolled drivers. Finance wants top-up per trip. Each wants its own number. How do you get to one, and what do you say to the teams who lose?" | "Driver operations says hours are what they control. Why not pay for those?" · "What if the teams still disagree after your answer?" |
| AI variant: "Your team ships an AI coding assistant. Sales quotes the share of suggestions developers accept. How would you measure whether the assistant is a success?" | "What would change your mind?" |

### Tuesday, one slide and a number from nothing

| Question, as the interviewer says it | Follow-ups to choose from |
|---|---|
| [S] "Here is the Hyderabad peak-hour guarantee case. You have two minutes with the city head, who is not technical. Present your recommendation." | "The finance partner says it is the monsoon. Answer in under a minute." · "Test or rollout? And how do you stop drivers moving into the zones you treat?" |
| [S] "Roughly how many drivers must be online at the evening peak in a city like Hyderabad to keep rider wait under five minutes? Talk me through it." | "Give a range, and say which factor drives it." · "Your idle share is a guess. Which number in the case data would catch it?" |
| AI variant: "You are the analyst at a coding-assistant company. The sales deck quotes acceptance rate, the share of suggestions developers accept, at 32 percent (a figure invented for this question). The head of product thinks it is the right number for the team. | Question, as the interviewer says it | Follow-ups to choose from |
|---|---|
| [S] "A fulfilment centre has two years of weekly shipments and three forecasts for the coming festive season. How do you evaluate a forecast?" | "Suppose the festival moves by three weeks this year. What breaks?" · "You set the cushion on the weeks you scored. Isn't that circular?" |

## How is an answer scored?

Score each line 0, 1 or 2 as you listen, and add the seven for a total out of 14. The total is for
the pair's own talk and is never handed in.

| Line | 0 | 1 | 2 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Clarified the scope | Starts on numbers at once | Asks one question, or asks and then ignores the answer | Asks what the number is for, who decides and what counts as harm, then builds on the answers |
| Named the denominator and the window | A number with no "per" and no period | One of the two | Both, said aloud, with the unit |
| Saw the paradox | Takes the headline at face value | Says something is off, with no number | Names it with numbers, such as hours up 24 percent while trips per online hour fell from 1.90 to 1.58 |
| Sized it with a unit | No size | A size with no unit or no base | A size with its unit and its base: orders a week, rupees a month or shipments a week |
| Said what would change their mind | Nothing | "More data" | A condition someone could test, with its number |
| Tied it to a decision and its cost | No decision | A decision with no cost, or a cost with no decision | The decision, who signs it, and what being wrong costs |
| Gave the structure before the number | Numbers first and no structure | The structure after the numbers | The structure first, then the numbers in its order |

A 2 also survives the follow-up: the answer to it uses a line of the definition or a number from the
case, never a new opinion.
