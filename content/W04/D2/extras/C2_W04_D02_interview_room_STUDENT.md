# How does a strong candidate present the redesign, and size drivers at the peak, out loud?

Written for this programme in the style of product-company and GCC screens; the facts inside are from
the case file and the day's data. It holds two transcripts and one AI-company variant. No
transcript claims to reproduce any company's real interview.

> Illustrative case: the company is real, the situation and the data are synthetic, built for this class. The coding-assistant company in the third transcript is invented.

**Who needs the answer.** You, rehearsing aloud before a screen that asks you to present a finding or
size a number on the spot.

**The questions on the way.**

- How do you present a recommendation to a non-technical leader in two minutes?
- How do you size drivers at the evening peak, aloud?
- How does the same presentation sound at an AI company?

## How do you present a recommendation to a non-technical leader in two minutes?

**The question, as the interviewer says it.** "Here is the Hyderabad peak-hour guarantee case. You
have two minutes with the city head, who is not technical. Present your recommendation."

**The clarifying questions, and the answers.**

| The candidate asks | The interviewer answers |
|---|---|
| What decision is the city head making? | Whether to keep the guarantee as it is, end it or change it |
| Who else hears it? | The driver-operations lead, the marketing lead and the finance partner |
| Is two minutes the whole slot? | Two minutes, then questions |

**The structure.** The decision; the claim; one number with its denominator and its window; what
riders got and where the money went; the caveat; the action with its cost and who signs; what would
change the candidate's mind; then the number others will quote, checked.

**The trap the interviewer fishes for.** Opening on method and reading out the dashboard, or quoting
the read that flatters the scheme: bookings per driver-week rose, which sounds like success.
Explaining that rise wrongly is as costly as missing it.

| A weak answer | A hire-grade answer |
|---|---|
| "So I looked at the dashboard. Peak online hours are up 24 percent and trips are up 3 percent. Trips per hour fell from 1.90 to 1.58, acceptance fell from 86 to 73 percent, and I worked out bookings three ways. Bookings per driver-week is actually up, so the picture is mixed. I think we should probably look at the scheme again, maybe pay per trip, but there is a lot going on, including the monsoon." | "You are deciding whether to keep, end or change the guarantee, so my answer first: the scheme is paying for online hours that carry no trips, and I recommend testing a change before the city pays for another six weeks. In the six weeks after the launch on 31 August, against the six weeks before, an hour a driver spends online at peak brought in Rs 260 of fares, down from Rs 314, at the same Rs 165 fare. Riders got little for the Rs 1.9 crore of top-up: the median wait went from 4.8 to 4.6 minutes, at least Rs 2,836 for each extra trip worth Rs 165. Rs 1.16 crore of it went to drivers who accepted fewer than half the rides offered to them, three-quarters of that in the three outer zones, where riders still wait about six minutes. One caveat: the monsoon ended in the same weeks and we have no weeks from last year, so part of the fall may be the season. My recommendation: pay per completed peak trip with an acceptance floor, tested first on half the drivers, picked at random, for six weeks. City-wide, that saves Rs 58 lakh to Rs 92.7 lakh over six weeks if a half to a fifth of that Rs 1.16 crore is still paid to drivers who start accepting. You sign the test. If it shows completed trips per driver falling, or the wait in any zone rising, I would drop the change. You will hear that bookings per driver-week rose 0.8 percent: it did, because each driver stayed online about four more hours a week while each hour earned 17 percent less, so the scheme bought hours, and the 140 new drivers pulled that number down." |

**Three follow-ups, with the line a hire-grade answer gives.**

1. "Which denominator did you choose, and what would make you choose another?" "Per peak online hour, because the scheme pays per online hour. If it paid per trip, I would read completed trips per enrolled driver, Monday's north star, and top-up per completed trip, Rs 82.3 after the launch; bookings per trip is only the fare."
2. "Test or rollout? And how do you stop drivers moving into the zones you treat?" "A test. I would pick drivers at random, never zones, because drivers move between zones: half the enrolled drivers on the new rule for six weeks, deciding on completed trips per enrolled driver, with the median wait in each zone as the guardrail, both fixed before it starts. Monday left the per-trip rate open, so the test also sets it."
3. "The finance partner says it is the monsoon. Answer in under a minute." "You may be right about part of it, and we have no weeks from last year. Weekly requests did fall four weeks running from 7 September, but in the last three weeks they were back inside the before window's range, and trips per hour still ran at 1.56 against 1.90 before. The comparison that settles it is a city where the rains ended and no scheme launched; if its trips per hour fell as far, I would change my view."

## How do you size drivers at the evening peak, aloud?

**The question, as the interviewer says it.** "Roughly how many drivers must be online at the evening
peak in a city like Hyderabad to keep rider wait under five minutes? Talk me through it."

**The clarifying questions, and the answers.**

| The candidate asks | The interviewer answers |
|---|---|
| Online at one moment, or counted across the window? | At one moment |
| Which window, and how long is it? | The weekday evening peak, three hours |
| Which product? | One product; assume cars |
| Is five minutes the median for the city or for every zone? | The city's median; say what you would do about zones |
| Do drivers run two apps? | Some do |

**The structure.** Clarify; say the formula before any number (drivers online equals trips to serve
in an hour, divided by the trips one online driver completes in an hour, times a buffer); calculate
aloud with a unit beside every factor; give the range with everything else held; validate like for
like and move the answer if the check says so; tie the number to the decision and its cost.

**The trap the interviewer fishes for.** A day's trips divided by an hour's rate, and a check that
compares unlike quantities.

| A weak answer | A hire-grade answer |
|---|---|
| "Hyderabad has about 1 crore people. Say 1 percent take a ride, so 1 lakh trips. A driver does about 2.4 trips an hour, so you need about 41,700 drivers." | "I will size drivers online at one moment of the three-hour weekday evening peak, one product, with the city's median wait under five minutes. Drivers online equals trips to serve in an hour, divided by the trips one online driver completes in an hour, times a buffer for the busiest half hour. Trips: about 1 crore people take about 1 Uber trip per 100 people on a weekday, so 1 lakh trips, and a quarter fall in the three evening hours, about 8,330 trips an hour; an hour's trips go over an hour's rate, never the day's. A busy driver fits 2.4 trips an hour, a 25-minute trip, and about 30 percent of online drivers have no rider at a given moment, so each online driver completes about 1.68 an hour. 8,330 divided by 1.68 is about 4,960, and 15 percent more for the busiest half hour gives about 5,700. With everything else held, trips per person moves it most, from about 2,900 to 8,600. The five-minute target enters through the idle share: idle drivers spread across the city are what make a short wait possible. To check, I compare like with like: the case's zones used 0.53 online driver-hours per completed trip before the launch, when the median wait was 4.8 minutes, against my 0.60, so my chain runs about an eighth high, and at the case's 1.90 trips an hour the answer is about 5,000. So I would say 5,000 to 5,700, and ask for Uber's own count of drivers online at peak to replace the guess. That number sets the evening incentive: every thousand extra drivers held online for the three hours with no trips costs up to Rs 9 lakh an evening at the Rs 300 floor." |

**Three follow-ups, with the line a hire-grade answer gives.**

1. "Your idle share is a guess. Which number in the case data would catch it?" "The case counts hours with no completed trip, 22 percent before the launch and 38 percent after, which is looser than idle at a moment. Like for like, its trips per hour and my 25-minute trip give an idle share of about 0.21 before and 0.34 after. The before value held the wait at 4.8 minutes, so it is the one my target needs, and it gives about 5,000."
2. "The window is three hours. What does a four-hour window do?" "Nothing at the busiest moment. The average hour falls to about 6,250 trips if the fourth hour adds none, so the buffer for the same busiest half hour rises to about 1.53 and the answer stays about 5,700; a fourth hour would also bring trips of its own."
3. "Give a range, and say which factor drives it." "About 2,900 to 8,600 with every other factor at its central value; trips per person drives it. With every factor at its bad end at once the range is far wider, so the first number I would replace is trips per person, with Uber's own peak trips an hour."

## How does the same presentation sound at an AI company?

**The question, as the interviewer says it.** "You are the analyst at a coding-assistant company. The
sales deck quotes acceptance rate, the share of suggestions developers accept, at 32 percent (a figure
invented for this question). The head of product thinks it is the right number for the team. Make
your case in two minutes; they will push back."

**The clarifying questions, and the answers.**

| The candidate asks | The interviewer answers |
|---|---|
| What is the team judged on today? | Acceptance rate |
| Does a suggestion count as accepted if it is deleted later? | Yes |
| What is the business paid on? | Seat renewals |
| Can we hold some users out of a change? | Yes, by user |

**The structure.** The claim; the proposed north star with its unit, denominator and window; how it
splits into factors; the guardrails that catch padding, and the cost; the true part of the objection,
conceded, with its evidence; the test and the result that would change the candidate's mind.

**The trap the interviewer fishes for.** Dropping acceptance outright, which ignores that customers
buy on it, or keeping it as the north star, which rewards text accepted and then deleted; and a
retention window that differs for every developer.

| A weak answer | A hire-grade answer |
|---|---|
| "Acceptance rate is a vanity metric, so we should stop reporting it and survey developers about whether they like the tool." | "Acceptance counts a suggestion as a win even when the developer deletes it soon after, so it can rise while the product helps less. At the deck's 32 percent, if a fifth of accepted characters are gone within a week, only about 26 percent of what we showed survives (invented numbers). I propose the team be judged on characters of suggested code still in the file seven days after acceptance, per developer who saw at least one suggestion that week. It splits into suggestions shown, times the share accepted, times characters per accepted suggestion, times the share still there after seven days, so when it moves we can see which factor moved. Two guardrails catch padding: retained characters per accepted character, and edits per accepted suggestion; serving cost per developer-week sits beside them, since showing more suggestions costs compute. You are right that customers see acceptance, and a 2022 study of GitHub Copilot's users found that acceptance, more than whether the code stays, tracks how productive developers feel, so acceptance stays in the deck as a leading input with its definition printed. Before anyone is judged on the new number, we hold out a share of users for a quarter and keep whichever number tracks seat renewals better. If acceptance tracks renewals and retained code does not, I would keep acceptance as the north star and add retention as a guardrail." |

**Three follow-ups, with the line a hire-grade answer gives.**

1. "What if retained code falls when we ship a faster model?" "Read the four factors. If suggestions shown rose and the share retained fell, the model is padding; if suggestions shown fell, developers are writing more themselves. A test of the new model against the old one, by user, settles which."
2. "Isn't retained code easy to game too?" "Long boilerplate that compiles and is never reverted would pass checks on build failures and revert rate, which is why the guardrails are ratios: retained characters per accepted character, and edits per accepted suggestion. Padding raises characters accepted without raising what survives per character."
3. "Why not just ask developers whether it helps?" "Because self-report misleads. METR's randomised study of 16 experienced open-source developers, published 10 July 2025, found tasks took 19 percent longer with AI tools while the developers believed they were 20 percent faster, and METR has since said those results are out of date. A holdout read on renewals measures what the business is paid on."
