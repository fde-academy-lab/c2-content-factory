# How does a strong candidate present the redesign, and size drivers at the peak, out loud?

Written for this programme in the style of product-company and global capability centre (GCC) screens; the facts inside are from
the case file and the day's data. It holds three transcripts, the third an AI-company variant. No
transcript claims to reproduce any company's real interview.

> Illustrative case: the company is real, the situation and the data are synthetic, built for this class. The coding-assistant company in the third transcript is invented.

**Who needs the answer.** You, rehearsing aloud before a screen that asks you to present a finding or
size a number on the spot.

**The questions on the way.**

- How do you present a recommendation to a non-technical leader in two minutes?
- How do you size drivers at the evening peak, aloud?
- How does the same presentation sound at an AI company?

---

## How do you present a recommendation to a non-technical leader in two minutes?

**The question, as the interviewer says it.** "Here is the Hyderabad peak-hour guarantee case. You
have two minutes with the city head, who is not technical. Present your recommendation."

**The clarifying questions, and the answers.**

| The candidate asks | The interviewer answers |
|---|---|
| What decision is the city head making? | Whether to keep the guarantee as it is, end it or change it |
| Who else hears it? | The driver-operations lead, the marketing lead and the finance partner |
| Is two minutes the whole slot? | Two minutes, then questions |
| Which weeks does the city head compare? | The six weeks before the launch on 31 August against the six weeks after |

**The structure.** The decision; the claim; one number with its denominator and its window; what
riders got and where the money went; the caveat; the action with its cost and who signs; what would
change the candidate's mind; then the number others will quote, checked.

**The trap the interviewer fishes for.** Opening on method and reading out the dashboard, or quoting
the read that flatters the scheme: bookings per driver-week rose, which sounds like success.
Explaining that rise wrongly is as costly as missing it.

| A weak answer | A hire-grade answer |
|---|---|
| "So I looked at the dashboard. Peak online hours are up 24 percent and trips are up 3 percent. Trips per hour fell from 1.90 to 1.58, acceptance fell from 86 to 73 percent, and I worked out bookings three ways. Bookings per driver-week is actually up, so the picture is mixed. I think we should probably look at the scheme again, maybe pay per trip, but there is a lot going on, including the monsoon." | "You are deciding whether to keep, end or change the guarantee, so my answer first: the scheme is paying for online hours that carry no trips, and I recommend testing a change before the city pays for another six weeks. Comparing the six weeks since the launch on 31 August with the six weeks before, an hour a driver spends online at peak brought in Rs 260 of fares, down from Rs 314, at the same Rs 165 fare. Riders got little for the Rs 1.9 crore of top-up: the median wait went from 4.8 to 4.6 minutes. Rs 1.16 crore of it went to drivers who accepted fewer than half the rides offered to them, three-quarters of that in the three outer zones, where the wait fell most, from 6.6 to about 6 minutes, so that is the wait I would watch. One caveat: the monsoon ended in the same weeks and we have no weeks from last year, so part of the fall may be the season. My recommendation: pay per completed peak trip with an acceptance floor, tested on half the drivers, picked at random, for six weeks, at a per-trip rate fixed before it starts. Rolled out to every enrolled driver, Monday's arithmetic saves Rs 58 lakh to Rs 92.7 lakh over six weeks if a half to a fifth of that money is still paid, and the rate can move it within that range or beyond it. You sign the test. I would stop it if the test half completes fewer trips per driver than the other half, or if any zone's median wait over the six weeks runs more than half a minute above its six weeks before the launch. You will hear that bookings per driver-week rose 0.8 percent: those are longer weeks, mostly drivers who already accept working about 4 percent more hours at the same rate per hour, while each hour across the city earned 17 percent less." |

**Three follow-ups, with the line a hire-grade answer gives.**

1. "Which denominator did you choose, and what would make you choose another?" "Per peak online hour, because the scheme pays per online hour. Per driver-week flatters it: that read rose on the other 880 drivers who were there before the launch, who drove about 4 percent more hours at an unchanged Rs 313.5 an hour, while the 180 who accept least doubled their hours and brought in 12 percent less in fares. If it paid per trip, I would read completed trips per enrolled driver, Monday's north star, and top-up per completed trip, Rs 82.3 after the launch; bookings per trip is only the fare."
2. "Test or rollout? And how do you stop drivers moving into the zones you treat?" "A test, with drivers picked at random, never zones, because drivers move between zones: half the enrolled drivers on the new rule for six weeks, at a per-trip rate fixed before the start, deciding on completed trips per enrolled driver. Both halves serve the same riders, so a driver on the new rule who accepts more takes trips a driver on the old rule would have done, and the gap between the halves flatters the change; I would say so on the slide and read the city's totals beside it, and if the city head wants a cleaner answer, alternate the whole city between the two rules week by week. The zone waits are shared by both halves, so they guard the city rather than compare the halves: the change stops if any zone's median wait, read over the test's six weeks, runs more than half a minute above its six weeks before the launch, since a single week moves on noise."
3. "The finance partner says it is the monsoon. Answer in under a minute." "You may be right about part of it, and we have no weeks from last year. Weekly requests did fall four weeks running from 7 September, but in the last three weeks they were back inside the before window's range, and trips per hour still ran at 1.56 against 1.90 before. The fall also sits in one group: the 880 who kept accepting held 1.90 trips an hour while the 180 fell to 0.80, and both ran 1.90 before the launch. The 180 were picked by how they did after the launch, so that shows where the fall sits; what settles why is a city where the rains ended and no scheme launched, and if its trips per hour fell as far, I would change my view."

## How do you size drivers at the evening peak, aloud?

**The question, as the interviewer says it.** "Roughly how many drivers must be online at the evening
peak in a city like Hyderabad to keep rider wait under five minutes? Talk me through it."

**The clarifying questions, and the answers.**

| The candidate asks | The interviewer answers |
|---|---|
| Online at one moment, or counted across the window? | At one moment |
| Which window, and how long is it? | The weekday evening peak, three hours |
| Which product? | One product; assume cars |
| Is five minutes the median for the city or for every zone? | The city's median; say what you would do about zones |
| Do drivers run two apps? | Some do |

**The structure.** Clarify; say the formula before any number (drivers online equals trips to serve
in an hour, divided by the trips one online driver completes in an hour, times a buffer); calculate
aloud with a unit beside every factor; give the range with everything else held; validate like for
like and move the answer if the check says so; tie the number to the decision and its cost.

**The trap the interviewer fishes for.** A day's trips divided by an hour's rate, and a check that
compares unlike quantities.

| A weak answer | A hire-grade answer |
|---|---|
| "Hyderabad has about 1 crore people. Say 1 percent take a ride, so 1 lakh trips. A driver does about 2.4 trips an hour, so you need about 41,700 drivers." | "I will size drivers online at one moment of the three-hour weekday evening peak, one product, with the city's median wait under five minutes; the three outer zones get few requests, so I would size them on their own. Drivers online equals trips to serve in an hour, divided by the trips one online driver completes in an hour, times a buffer for the busiest half hour. Trips: about 1 crore people. Most take no app ride on a given weekday, a rider usually goes out and back, and an Uber car is one choice among autos, bikes and other apps, so I take about 1 Uber trip per 100 people on a weekday, 1 lakh trips, and give that guess the widest range, a half to one and a half. A quarter of them fall in the three evening hours, about 8,330 trips an hour; an hour's trips go over an hour's rate, never the day's. A peak trip takes about 25 minutes from accepting to dropping, a guess I would check first against the app's trip times, so a busy driver fits 2.4 an hour. About 30 percent of online drivers have no Uber rider at a given moment, more if many run two apps, which would push the answer up, so each online driver completes about 1.68 an hour. 8,330 divided by 1.68 is about 4,960, and 15 percent more for the busiest half hour gives about 5,700. With everything else held, trips per person moves it most, from about 2,900 to 8,600. The five-minute target enters through the idle share: idle drivers spread across the city are what make a short wait possible. To check, I compare like with like. Monday's data is a slice, the scheme's enrolled drivers, about 581 of them online in an average peak hour after the launch, so its count cannot check the city's, but one ratio can: they used 0.53 online driver-hours per completed trip before the launch, when the median wait was 4.8 minutes, pooled over both daily peaks on all seven days, against my 0.60. So my chain runs about an eighth high, and at the case's 1.90 trips an hour the answer is about 5,000. I would say about 5,000 to 5,700 where the two routes agree, inside an honest range of 2,900 to 8,600 set by how often people ride, and the first number I would replace is trips per person, with Uber's own peak trips an hour. That number sets the evening incentive: every thousand extra drivers held online for the three hours with no trips costs up to Rs 9 lakh an evening at the Rs 300 floor." |

**Three follow-ups, with the line a hire-grade answer gives.**

1. "Your idle share is a guess. Which number in the case data would catch it?" "The case counts hours with no completed trip, 22 percent before the launch and 38 percent after, which is looser than idle at a moment. The check that needs no guess is the ratio itself: 0.53 online driver-hours per completed trip before the launch, when the wait held at 4.8 minutes, against my 0.60, which gives about 5,000. Turned into an idle share with my 25-minute trip it is about 0.21 before and 0.34 after, but that step leans on the same trip-length guess, so I quote the ratio and keep the share as a reading of it."
2. "The window is three hours. What does a four-hour window do?" "Nothing at the busiest moment. The average hour falls to about 6,250 trips if the fourth hour adds none, so the buffer for the same busiest half hour rises to about 1.53 and the answer stays about 5,700; a fourth hour would also bring trips of its own."
3. "Give a range, and say which factor drives it." "About 2,900 to 8,600 with every other factor at its central value; trips per person drives it. With every factor at its bad end at once the range is far wider, so the first number I would replace is trips per person, with Uber's own peak trips an hour."

## How does the same presentation sound at an AI company?

**The question, as the interviewer says it.** "You are the analyst at a coding-assistant company. The
sales deck quotes acceptance rate, the share of suggestions developers accept, at 32 percent (a figure
invented for this question). The head of product thinks it is the right number for the team. Make
your case in two minutes; they will push back."

**The clarifying questions, and the answers.**

| The candidate asks | The interviewer answers |
|---|---|
| What is the team judged on today? | Acceptance rate |
| Does a suggestion count as accepted if it is deleted later? | Yes |
| What is the business paid on, and how often does a customer decide? | Seats, renewed by each customer account once a year |
| Can the editor tell how much accepted code is still there a week later, and since when? | Yes: it counts each suggestion's characters still in the file, without sending the code, and has logged that for two years; a few accounts switch even that off |

**The structure.** The claim; the proposed north star with its unit, denominator and window; how it
splits into factors; the guardrails that catch padding, and the cost; the true part of the objection,
conceded, with its evidence; the check against renewals and the result that would change the
candidate's mind.

**The trap the interviewer fishes for.** Dropping acceptance outright, which ignores that customers
buy on it, or keeping it as the north star, which rewards text accepted and then deleted; a
denominator that shrinks when developers give up on the tool; and multiplying shares of different
things, suggestions by characters, into one percentage.

| A weak answer | A hire-grade answer |
|---|---|
| "Acceptance rate is a vanity metric, so we should stop reporting it and survey developers about whether they like the tool." | "Acceptance counts a suggestion as a win even when the developer deletes it soon after, so it can rise while the product helps less. At the deck's 32 percent, if a fifth of accepted suggestions are deleted within a week, only about 26 of every 100 suggestions we show survive (invented numbers). I propose the team be judged on characters of suggested code still in the file seven days after acceptance, per paid seat a week: a seat is what the customer pays for, so a developer who switches the tool off still counts, and the number falls when they do. It splits into suggestions shown per seat, times the share accepted, times characters per accepted suggestion, times the share of those characters still there after seven days, so when it moves we can see which factor moved. Padding shows first in characters per accepted suggestion, so that is one guardrail, with a ceiling agreed before launch; the second is a monthly sample of surviving suggestions that engineers read and mark as useful or boilerplate; serving cost per seat-week sits beside them, since showing more suggestions costs compute. The editor counts each suggestion's characters still in the file without sending the code, so a suggestion the developer edits counts for the part that stays, and the few accounts that switch even that off are reported apart. You are right that customers see acceptance, and a 2022 study of GitHub Copilot's users found that acceptance, more than whether the code stays, tracks how productive developers feel, so acceptance stays in the deck as a leading input with its definition printed. Before anyone is judged on the new number, I would check it against what we are paid on: accounts renew once a year, so across last year's renewals, did surviving code per seat separate the accounts that renewed or added seats from those that cut them better than acceptance did? If acceptance separates them and surviving code does not, I would keep acceptance as the north star and add survival as a guardrail." |

**Three follow-ups, with the line a hire-grade answer gives.**

1. "What if retained code falls when we ship a faster model?" "Read the four factors. If suggestions shown per seat rose while the share accepted fell, the model shows more that developers reject; if characters per accepted suggestion rose while the share still there fell, it is padding; if suggestions shown fell, developers are writing more themselves. A test of the new model against the old one, by account, settles which."
2. "Isn't retained code easy to game too?" "Yes: long boilerplate that compiles and is never reverted survives a week, so survival alone cannot catch padding. Padding shows first in characters per accepted suggestion, which jumps when the model pads, so that guardrail has a ceiling agreed before launch, and the monthly sample read by engineers says whether what survives is useful or boilerplate."
3. "Why not just ask developers whether it helps?" "Because how developers feel and what they get can point opposite ways. In METR's randomised study of 16 experienced open-source developers, published 10 July 2025, tasks took 19 percent longer with AI tools allowed while the developers believed they had been 20 percent faster. It timed whole tasks of about two hours, never completions, and METR's page now says those results are out of date, so I cite it for one point only: a feeling is not a measure. The business is paid on seat renewals, which is why the check runs against them."
