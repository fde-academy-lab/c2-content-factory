# What does a one-page case the city head can use look like?

This file is released after the pages are handed in. The page below is one way to pass all five of
chapter 7's rules; yours can pass them in other words. The case: Uber's peak-hour guarantee in
Hyderabad, launched on 31 August 2026.

## What does a model page say?

> **To the city head, before Thursday's city review.**
>
> The peak-hour guarantee is paying for online hours that carry no trips, so I recommend testing a
> change before the city pays for another six weeks.
>
> An hour a driver spends online at peak brought in Rs 314 of fares in the six weeks before the launch
> and Rs 260 in the six weeks after, while the average fare stayed at Rs 165, so the whole fall is
> fewer trips in each online hour. Riders gained little: the median wait went from 4.8 to 4.6 minutes.
> Most of the money went to a small group: Rs 1.16 crore of the Rs 1.9 crore top-up was paid for
> weeks in which the driver accepted fewer than half the rides offered, three-quarters of it in the
> three outer zones.
>
> One thing the numbers cannot yet rule out: the monsoon ended in the same six weeks, and we do not
> have last year's weeks to compare, so part of the fall may be the season. In the last three weeks,
> with rider requests back at their level before the launch, trips per hour stayed low, which points
> away from the season, but only a test can settle it.
>
> I recommend paying the guarantee per completed peak trip, with a floor on how many offered rides a
> driver accepts, first for half the enrolled drivers, picked at random, for six weeks, at a per-trip
> rate fixed before the test starts. Across the enrolled drivers, Monday's arithmetic puts the saving
> at Rs 58 lakh over six weeks if half of that Rs 1.16 crore is still paid to drivers who start
> accepting, and Rs 92.7 lakh if a fifth is, and the rate decides where in that range it lands; you
> sign the test, and we drop the change if completed trips per driver fall or the median wait in any
> zone rises above its level before the launch.

## Why does it pass each rule?

Typed one part a line, as notebook 09 asks, the page passes all five of `page_check`'s rules.

| Rule | Where the page meets it |
|---|---|
| 1. The claim first, with what to do and why | "The peak-hour guarantee is paying for online hours that carry no trips, so I recommend testing a change before the city pays for another six weeks." |
| 2. One number, what it is divided by and its window | Rs 314 to Rs 260 of fares per online hour at peak, six weeks either side of the launch |
| 3. The caveat before the action | The third paragraph, in full sentences |
| 4. The action, its cost in rupees and weeks, and who signs | The last paragraph: the test, the saving range with its assumption and the rate it waits on, the city head, and the result that would stop the change |
| 5. No method word | It says "fares per online hour", never "denominator" or "ratio" |

The page asks the city head to sign a test first, which is what the caveat calls for: only a test can
separate the scheme from the season. The test splits drivers at random, one by one, because a driver
moves between zones and a split by zone would put the same driver on both sides. Both halves still
serve the same riders, so a driver on the new rule who accepts more takes trips a driver on the old
rule would have done, and the comparison between the halves flatters the change; the city's own
totals are read beside it, and a test that alternates the whole city between the two rules, week by
week, avoids the overlap at the cost of a longer run.

## Which mistakes come back most often?

The most common are a page that opens on method ("I analysed the dashboard"); a number with no denominator ("bookings
fell 17 percent"); a caveat in a bracket at the end; a saving with no assumption behind it; and the
per-driver-week rise quoted as good news, which the morning showed came from more online hours at 17
percent less per hour, most of them in the idle tail, whose 180 drivers doubled their hours.
