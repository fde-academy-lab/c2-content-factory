# Which letters answer the chapter 1 set on the one-slide answer, and why?

Answers: 1c 2d 3b 4a 5b

The worlds: an auto driver (invented) who earned Rs 1,800 on both days, from 20 rides in 9 hours and
then 18 rides in 12 hours; and Ramesh's kirana shop (invented), up Rs 700 a day and down 11.8 percent an

## What does the set test?

The four parts of the one-slide answer, each on its own: a title that acts, evidence read in the unit
the decision buys, a caveat that names what the evidence cannot show yet, and an action with its cost
and its owner. Item 5 hands you a plausible wrong slide to debug.

## Why is each key right, and each other letter wrong?

| Item | Key | The question in one line | Why the key holds | Why each other option fails |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | c | Which title tells the auto driver what to do? | It is a sentence with a verb that says what is true (the same rupees in three more hours) and what to do (drive the short day). | a names a topic. b lists true numbers and leaves the reader to find the point. d is a topic again, made longer. |
| 2 | d | Which number is the evidence for whether to drive the long day again? | The question is about hours, so it is read per hour: Rs 200 an hour against Rs 150. | a is flat, so it hides the three extra hours. b rose, which flatters the long day, and rides are not what he spends. c counts rides, not what an hour earns. |
| 3 | b | Which line is the caveat? | It names what the evidence cannot show yet: how much of the extra Rs 700 is the season. | a is evidence beside the claim. c is the action. d is the evidence itself. |
| 4 | a | Which action can Ramesh sign? | It says what to do, for how long, what it costs (Rs 170 a day) and who signs. | b has no window, no cost and no owner. c has no date and no owner, and waits for nothing named. d has a window but no cost and no owner. |
| 5 | b | What is wrong with "sales a day rose, so the hours work"? | The decision is about two extra hours, so it is priced per hour, where the shop earns 11.8 percent less. | a: a true number can still answer the wrong question. c: per customer is flat and answers whether customers spent less, a different question. d: a chart adds evidence, and the title still has to say what to do. |

## Which item is worth arguing about?

Item 2, option b. Rupees a ride rose from Rs 90 to Rs 100, which makes the long day look better. The
driver sells his hours, though, and per hour the long day earned Rs 50 less; per ride belongs on the
appendix page for whoever asks about fares.

## Where does the pattern live at work?

Every review slide built from a dashboard: the analyst picks the denominator the decision is priced in,
writes the claim as the title, puts the caveat beside the number and ends on an action with its cost.
Chapter 4 does it on Uber's twelve driver-weeks, where per hour falls 37 percent, per driver-week 23
percent and per trip not at all.
