## The deliberate failure

Someone will propose a solution within the first few minutes, usually the app. Write it on the board
under a heading that reads "proposed before we knew", say nothing else about it, and go on answering
questions. At the end of the twenty minutes, go back to it and ask the room what they did not know
when it was proposed. The answer is most of the fact sheet.

## The fact sheet: answer only what is asked

The case is invented for this class, and the weekend project's register matches it. Give a fact only
when a question reaches it, and give no more than the question asked for.

| If they ask about | The warden or the mess manager says |
|---|---|
| How many students eat in the mess | 240 students are registered, and dinner is served every night of the week |
| How much is cooked | The mess manager orders dinner for all 240 every night, because nobody knows who will come |
| How the vendor is paid | By the plate ordered, eaten or not; about Rs 40 a plate, which is a figure invented for this case |
| When the vendor needs the order | The day before |
| What happens to leftovers | They are thrown away |
| Whether there are records | The mess manager keeps a register of plates eaten each night, and has the last four weeks |
| How sure the "fifth" is | It is the warden's estimate. If someone asks what the register says: about 22 percent over the four weeks |
| Which nights are worst | The weekends, when students go home or out. If someone asks for the numbers: Friday to Sunday carry about 63 percent of the waste |
| Whether students say when they will skip | A few tell the mess manager informally; there is no rule and no form |
| Who must agree to a new rule for students | The mess committee, which is run by students and meets once a month |
| Where the app idea came from | A parent who saw one at another hostel |
| What success would look like | A smaller mess bill, without complaints that food ran out |
| The budget for an app | None has been set aside |
| How students can be reached | Every student is on the hostel's messaging group |

Anything not on the sheet gets "I don't know; I'd have to find out", which is itself a good answer for
the room to hear: it marks a must ask.

## The interview angle, with the answers

The three questions are the tracker's, from the row this class came from. The answers are for the
team.

**[S] A client says 'we need an AI app'; what do you ask before you propose anything?** A strong
answer asks what the app is meant to change and how the client would know it changed, who decides and
who pays and who lives with the result, what number would move and by how much for it to matter, and
what is already known against what is only assumed. It names at least one question whose answer could
make the app unnecessary.

**[F] How do you tell the stated problem from the real one?** The stated problem usually names a
solution ("an app that predicts"). The real one names the pain and its cause in the owner's terms
("food is ordered with no signal of who is coming"). A good test: the real problem can be solved in
more than one way, and at least one of those ways has no model in it.

**[D] The client has already chosen the solution and it is the wrong one; how do you open that
conversation?** Start from their goal, never from the solution: agree the number that would tell them
it worked, show what the records say about where that number moves, and put the cheaper option beside
theirs on the same measures, so the comparison makes the case. Never lead with "that's wrong".
