# Which answers hold in the chapter 4 set on whether the monsoon discount worked, and why?

Answers: 1c 2a 3b 4d 5b 6a

Marketing reported that customers who got Kalpa Retail's monsoon sale, 15 percent off in August,
spent 6 percent more than customers who did not, and wants the sale again at Diwali. Chapter 4
rebuilt the figure, Rs 3,395 against Rs 3,200, a blended lift of 6.1 percent, and then split it by
segment: inside Retail-Plus and inside Retail-Core the customers who got the sale spent 3.0 percent
less, and the group who got it was 50 percent Retail-Plus against 40 percent of the rest. Retail-Plus
members spend more whatever the sale does, so the blend rose because of who got the sale. Three of
the six items are design items: 1, 3 and 5.

**Who needs the answer.** You, checking your six letters after the lab or tonight. Marketing will
defend the sale on Monday with the blended figure, and a reader who cannot say why a blend rises
while every segment falls hands them the Diwali budget.

**The questions on the way.**

- Which idea does this set test?
- Why is each key right, item by item?
- Which wrong answer is worth arguing about?
- Where does this show up at work?

## Which idea does this set test?

A comparison is only as fair as the groups in it. When two groups hold different mixes of customers,
a blended figure measures the mix as much as the campaign. The design items give new campaigns and
ask which comparison to run first and what the blend will show, which plan to put to Meera for a
deeper discount, and which set-up makes the next readout fair. The other items ask how large a lift
the mix alone manufactures, which figure the note reports, and what the one-mix route can and cannot
check.

## Why is each key right, item by item?

### Q1. Which comparison do you run first on the loyalty email, and what will the blend show?

A design item. An invented loyalty email went to 200 customers, 70 percent premium, and not to 300
others, 30 percent premium, and premium customers spend about three times as much.

The key is c, "Inside each tier, and expect the blend to look better than the tiers". The emailed
group is 70 percent premium against 30, and premium customers spend three times as much, so the
blend will flatter the email whatever it did. The fair comparison runs inside each tier.

- a, "The blend, and expect it to show the email's true effect": the blend mixes the email with who
  got it.
- b, "Before and after for the emailed group, and expect a rise": before and after credits the email
  with everything else that changed.
- d, "Nothing yet, and wait for a randomly assigned email in the next quarter": waiting leaves this
  email unread when the data can already answer.

### Q2. What lift does the blend report for a campaign whose exposed group is 60 percent premium?

Inside each of two invented tiers the exposed and unexposed spent the same, Rs 6,000 in premium and
Rs 2,000 in basic, and the exposed group was 60 percent premium against 30 percent.

The key is a, "37.5 percent, all of it from who got the campaign". Exposed: 0.6 of Rs 6,000 plus
0.4 of Rs 2,000 is Rs 4,400. Unexposed: 0.3 of Rs 6,000 plus 0.7 of Rs 2,000 is Rs 3,200. Rs 1,200
over Rs 3,200 is 37.5 percent, and every rupee of it is mix, since nothing changed inside either
tier.

- b, "37.5 percent, and the campaign should be credited with it": the right number read as the
  campaign's doing.
- c, "30 percent, the gap between the two premium shares": a gap in shares is points of mix, and
  points of mix are no lift in spend.
- d, "Zero, and Marketing's arithmetic must hold a mistake": the arithmetic is right; the groups are
  unequal.

### Q3. Which plan goes to Meera for a Diwali sale at 20 percent off?

A design item. Marketing proposes 20 percent off at Diwali and expects orders to rise 15 percent.

The key is b, "Hold back a random slice; repeat it only if orders rise over 25 percent". At
four-fifths of the price, revenue stands still only when orders rise by a quarter, so Marketing's own
15 percent would bring in about 8 percent less. A random hold-back measures the real lift, and the
plan names the 25 percent it has to beat.

- a, "Run it for everyone, since 15 percent more orders is a clear lift": forgets that each order
  now earns a fifth less, so a 15 percent rise loses revenue.
- c, "Run it, then set Diwali's revenue against the monsoon month's": before and after credits the
  discount with the festival.
- d, "Cut it to 10 percent off, where Marketing's 15 percent rise in orders would pay": the 15
  percent is Marketing's expectation for 20 percent off, and nobody has measured what any discount
  lifts.

### Q4. Which figure does the note report when the blend shows 8 percent more and each segment 2 percent less?

An invented campaign shows a blended lift of 8 percent, while inside each of its two segments the
customers who got it spent 2 percent less.

The key is d, "The segments' 2 percent less, with the mix as the blend's reason". Each segment
compares like with like, and the mix explains why the blend moved the other way.

- a, "The blended 8 percent, because it covers every customer the campaign reached": the chapter's
  wrong reading, in new numbers.
- b, "Both numbers side by side, for Meera to choose between": hands Meera the analysis in place of
  the answer.
- c, "The average of the three figures, to balance them": averaging a blend with its own parts has
  no meaning.

### Q5. Which set-up makes the next campaign's readout fair?

A design item. Marketing offers four set-ups for comparing customers who got the next campaign with
customers who did not, and asks which removes the mix problem.

The key is b, "Inside each segment, or with a coin deciding who gets the campaign". The two groups
differ in segment, so the fair set-up compares inside each segment, or lets a coin decide who gets
the campaign. Either way the groups being compared hold the same mix.

- a, "Exposed against unexposed customers inside the same city": a city holds both kinds of
  customer, so the segment mix stays inside every city.
- c, "Cities weighted by their order counts, then blended into one comparison": weighting cities
  leaves the segment mix inside each.
- d, "The same comparison as before, run on a sample ten times the size": a larger unfair comparison
  is still unfair.

### Q6. What does the one-mix route's agreement with the split check?

Putting both groups on one mix gives the same 3 percent less as the split by segment.

The key is a, "That the mix is the whole reason the blend rose". The one-mix route reuses the
split's four cells, the two segments' averages for each group, so it cannot disagree with the split.
What it checks is that the mix accounts for the whole of the blended lift.

- b, "That the four cells behind the split are free of errors": an error in the four cells would pass
  through both routes alike.
- c, "That the sale had no effect inside any segment": the split already says what happened inside
  each segment, and this route adds nothing there.
- d, "That a random hold-back would give the same answer": only a hold-back can say what a hold-back
  would show.

## Which wrong answer is worth arguing about?

Item 6, option b. Two routes agreeing usually means both are right, and some pairs will read it that
way. Here the second route is built from the same four numbers as the first, so it would agree with
them even if one of them were mistyped. Its job is to show the mix explains the blend; a check that
could catch a wrong cell has to come from another file, which is what chapter 6 does with Finance's
orders by month.

## Where does this show up at work?

UC Berkeley's 1973 graduate admissions looked biased against women in the total, 44 percent of men
admitted against 35 percent of women, while department by department about as many departments
favoured women as favoured men, because women applied more to the departments that admitted fewest. eBay found the
same shape in its search ads: new and infrequent users bought more after an ad, while frequent users,
who took most of the spend, did not, so the average hid the split. Every campaign readout that
compares customers who got it with customers who did not carries the same risk, and the split by
segment is the first check.
