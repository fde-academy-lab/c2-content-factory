# Solution: chapter 4 set, the discount split by segment

Answers: 1c 2a 3b 4d 5d 6a

## The idea being tested

A comparison is only as fair as the groups in it. When two groups hold different mixes of
customers, a blended figure measures the mix as much as the campaign. The design items ask which
comparison fits Meera's question, which fact would have made the blend fair, how large a lift the
mix alone can manufacture, what a discount must earn back, and when the one-mix route replaces the
split.

## The part worth arguing about

Item 5, option b. Showing both numbers feels transparent, and it leaves Meera to settle a question
the analyst has already settled. The note reports the segments' answer and names the mix as the
reason the blend rose, so the marketing lead sees that the arithmetic was right and the groups were
unequal.

## Where the pattern lives in production

UC Berkeley's 1973 graduate admissions looked biased against women in the total, while department
by department the bias ran slightly the other way: women applied more to the departments that
admitted fewest. Every campaign readout that compares "customers who got it" with "customers who did
not" has the same risk, and the split by segment is the first check.
