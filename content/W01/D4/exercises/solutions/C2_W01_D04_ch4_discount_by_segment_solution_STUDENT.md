# Solution: chapter 4 set, the discount split by segment

Answers: 1c 2a 3b 4d 5b 6a

## The idea being tested

A comparison is only as fair as the groups in it. When two groups hold different mixes of
customers, a blended figure measures the mix as much as the campaign. The design items give new
campaigns and ask which comparison to run and what the blend will show, how large a lift the mix
alone manufactures and what the note says about it, and what a deeper discount does to revenue. The
other items ask which figure the note reports, what fixes a mix problem, and what the one-mix route
can and cannot check.

## The part worth arguing about

Item 6, option b. Two routes agreeing usually means both are right, and some pairs will read it that
way. Here the second route is built from the same four numbers as the first, so it would agree with
them even if one of them were mistyped. Its job is to show the mix explains the blend; the check
that could catch a wrong cell has to come from another file, which is what chapter 6 does with
Finance's months.

## Where the pattern lives in production

UC Berkeley's 1973 graduate admissions looked biased against women in the total, while department
by department the bias ran slightly the other way: women applied more to the departments that
admitted fewest. eBay found the same shape in its search ads: new and infrequent users bought more
after an ad, while frequent users, who took most of the spend, did not, so the average hid the split.
Every campaign readout that compares "customers who got it" with "customers who did not" has the
same risk, and the split by segment is the first check.
