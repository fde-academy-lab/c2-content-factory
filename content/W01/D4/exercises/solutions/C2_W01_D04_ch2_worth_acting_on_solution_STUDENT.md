# Which answers hold in the chapter 2 set on whether the Retail-Plus fall is worth acting on, and why?

Answers: 1a 2c 3c 4b 5d 6a

Retail-Plus, Kalpa Retail's paid membership tier, fell Rs 1,110 a member from Q1 to Q2, and chapter
1 read that fall as borderline against chance: 0.029 of chance-only worlds made a fall that large and
0.057 a move that large either way. Chapter 2 asked whether the fall is big enough to act on. It is
worth Rs 24,420 a quarter, 0.19 percent of the company's Q2 delivered revenue; a Rs 500-a-member
retention offer for all 22 members costs Rs 11,000 and must win back 45 percent of the fall to pay
for itself, its break-even; and the range from resampling the members' own differences runs from
about Rs 80 to Rs 2,160 a member. Three of the six items are design items: 1, 3 and 5.

**Who needs the answer.** You, checking your six letters after the lab or tonight. The head of
Retail-Plus will ask for a budget on Monday, and a reply that confuses how sure with how big either
funds an offer that cannot pay back or waves away a fall that keeps draining the tier.

## Which idea does this set test?

Two calls, two measurements. A chance reference says whether a gap beats chance. Rupees against the
whole company and against the cost of acting say whether it deserves money. The design items give
new numbers and ask who gets the first test of an offer, what a range buys when its low end sits
below the cost, and what a measured recovery rate does to the decision. The other items ask what a
small share can and cannot say about size, how a review is ordered, and what a larger count does to a
share.

## Why is each key right, item by item?

### Q1. Which half of a 40-member tier gets the first test of a retention offer?

A design item. An invented tier of 40 members fell Rs 800 each; a Rs 300 offer must win back 37.5
percent to pay for itself; nobody has measured a recovery, and the head agrees to test on half first.

The key is a, "A coin-chosen 20, so the two halves start alike". A coin makes the two halves alike
before the offer, so any gap after it belongs to the offer. The test costs Rs 6,000 and leaves 20
members to compare with.

- b, "The 20 who fell most, since they have the most to win back": the members who fell most tend to
  drift back toward their own average whatever the offer does, so the offer would be credited with
  the drift.
- c, "The 20 with the lowest customer ids, which is close to random": ids follow whatever order the
  system gave them, which can track when members joined and how they spend.
- d, "The 20 who spent most last quarter, since they matter most": the heaviest spenders differ from
  the rest before the offer, so the comparison starts unfair.

### Q2. What does Kavya reply when the head of Retail-Plus calls the fall the company's biggest problem?

The head of Retail-Plus says the flips put the fall well outside the usual wobble, so it is the
company's biggest problem.

The key is c, "Disagree: size its rupees against the company first". The flips say how surely the
fall beats chance, and only its rupees set against the company and the cost of a fix say how big it
is. Here that is Rs 24,420 a quarter, 0.19 percent of the company.

- a, "Agree: a small share means the fall is the largest on the page": reads the smallest share as
  the biggest money.
- b, "Agree, once a second seed gives the same share": a second seed moves the share a little and
  leaves the size where it was.
- d, "Disagree: the share is borderline, so the fall did not happen": borderline is a reading of
  chance, and it never says the fall did not happen.

### Q3. What does a range of Rs 150 to Rs 1,900 a member buy against a Rs 400 offer?

A design item. An invented range for a tier's fall runs from Rs 150 to Rs 1,900 a member around an
estimate of Rs 1,000, and a retention offer costs Rs 400 a member.

The key is c, "A test on part of the tier first: the low end sits below the cost". The low end,
Rs 150, sits below the Rs 400 cost, so a full rollout can lose money. A test on a coin-chosen part
of the tier learns at part of the cost and leaves a comparison.

- a, "The offer for every member, because most of the range sits above the cost": the middle of the
  range hides its low end.
- b, "Nothing, because a range that wide supports no spending at all": the range supports a test,
  which is spending with a way to learn.
- d, "The offer at the estimate's break-even of 40 percent, for every member": a break-even on the
  estimate ignores how small the fall could be.

### Q4. In what order does the growth review take three findings with different rupees and shares?

Three invented findings: A moved Rs 5 lakh with a share of 0.40, B moved Rs 30,000 with 0.02, and C
moved Rs 2,000 with 0.001.

The key is b, "A, B, C: by rupees, each with its share said beside it". The review orders by money,
and says beside each move how often chance alone could make it.

- a, "C, B, A: the smallest share first, since it is the surest": surety read as size, the chapter's
  wrong ranking.
- c, "B, C, A: the two that beat chance first, then the rest": a share above 0.05 says the move could
  be the wobble, and it says nothing about whether the move matters.
- d, "A alone, since the other two are too small to discuss": B and C are small and still worth a
  line each.

### Q5. What does the budget meeting decide on a Rs 50,000 fix for a Rs 60,000 fall?

A design item. An invented tier's fall is Rs 60,000 a quarter, a fix costs Rs 50,000, and last year
the same fix on members like these won back 70 percent of a similar fall.

The key is d, "Not as priced: it needs 83 percent back and wins back 70". Rs 50,000 over Rs 60,000
means the fix must win back 83 percent to pay for itself. At last year's 70 percent it wins back
Rs 42,000 and loses about Rs 8,000 a quarter.

- a, "Fund it: 70 percent is a high recovery for any fix": a high rate still falls short of this
  fix's break-even.
- b, "Test it on half the tier first, since the rate is last year's": a measured rate is what lets
  the break-even decide without a test.
- c, "Fund it: the fall is large and the fix is proven": size and proof do not make a fix pay when
  its price is too high.

### Q6. What happens to a Rs 20 gap's share, and to the gap, when 100 orders a quarter become 20,000?

An invented Rs 20 gap between two quarters of different orders gives a share of 0.43 on 100 orders
a quarter.

The key is a, "The share falls toward zero, and the gap stays Rs 20". More orders narrow the wobble
chance makes, so the share falls toward zero while the Rs 20 stays Rs 20: on the chapter's run it
reads 0.002 at 20,000 orders. Significance grows with the count, and size does not.

- b, "The share stays near 0.43, while the gap grows with the orders": the gap is set at Rs 20 at
  every size.
- c, "Both fall, since the orders dilute the gap": the gap does not dilute.
- d, "The share rises, since more orders add more noise": more orders shrink the noise in an
  average.

## Which wrong answer is worth arguing about?

Item 5, option b. A test on half the tier sounds careful, and after chapter 2 some pairs reach for it
by habit. Here the measured rate already answers what a test would measure, and the arithmetic says
the fix loses money even at that rate. The honest line is "not as priced": the fix needs a cheaper
version, or a larger fall to address, before anyone tests it.

## Where does this show up at work?

At Microsoft, about a third of the experiments designed to improve a metric succeeded, and one change
to how Bing showed ad headlines raised revenue by more than 100 million dollars a year. What made the
Bing change worth acting on was its size in money, so an analyst writes the size and the cost of a
change beside every share before a backlog is ranked.
