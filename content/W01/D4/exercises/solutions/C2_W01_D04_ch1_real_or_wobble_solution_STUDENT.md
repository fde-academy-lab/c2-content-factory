# Which answers hold in the chapter 1 set on whether the Retail-Plus fall is real, and why?

Meera Raghavan, Kalpa Retail's CEO, asked whether the fall in Retail-Plus, Kalpa's paid membership
tier, is real or the wobble Kalpa sees every quarter. The same 22 members bought in both quarters,

## Which idea does this set test?

A chance reference builds worlds in which nothing changed and counts how often those worlds make a
gap as large as the real one, and the way the data was collected decides how the worlds are built:
the same members measured twice keep their pairs and flip them, and two groups of different
customers shuffle their labels. The design items give new data and ask which reference fits, when
the textbook paired test takes over, and what pooling does on a tier where heavy buyers stay heavy.
The other items ask the question every share raises: in which world, and in which direction, was it
counted?

## Why is each key right, item by item?

### Q1. Which chance reference fits the store team's request, and which fits the app team's?

A design item. The store team compares 300 customers who used self-checkout in September with 280
different customers who did not; the app team compares the same 45 members' spend in the month
before and the month after an app update.

The key is b, "Shuffle the labels for the store team, and flip each member's pair for the app team".
The store team's two groups are different customers, so their labels are shuffled; the app team
measured the same 45 members twice, so each member's pair is flipped.

- a, "Flip each member's pair for both requests, since both compare two stretches of spend": the
  store team's two groups share no members, so there are no pairs to flip.
- c, "Shuffle the labels for both, since each request compares two groups of numbers": shuffling the
  app team's 90 totals treats 45 people as 90 strangers.
- d, "Flip each pair for the store team, and shuffle the labels for the app team": swaps the two
  designs.

### Q2. What verdict does Kavya give the draft note's p-value sentence?

A draft note reads "p = 0.03, so there is a 3 percent chance our Retail-Plus finding is wrong."

The key is d, "Wrong: the share was counted in worlds where nothing changed". The share is counted
in worlds built under chance alone, so it cannot be the chance that a finding about the real world is
wrong.

- a, "Defensible, because 0.03 sits below the 0.05 line that most analysts use": 0.05 is a
  convention, and it says nothing about what the share means.
- b, "Defensible, if the flips used a fixed seed, since the share is then exact": a seed makes the
  run repeatable and leaves a wrong reading wrong.
- c, "Wrong in its wording only: it should say a 97 percent chance of being right": turning it into
  97 percent right repeats the same mistake from the other side.

### Q3. Which route should a team with 4,000 bell-shaped differences run?

A design item. A GCC team compares the same 4,000 members' monthly spend before and after a price
change; the differences look bell-shaped, and the team's dashboard reports the textbook paired test.

The key is c, "The textbook paired test, which matches the flips at this size and is the standard".
Four thousand pairs whose differences behave give the textbook paired test the same answer as the
flips in one line, and the team reads that test every week.

- a, "The flips, because they assume less and can be shown with cards to anyone": the flips would
  agree and add nothing a dashboard reader needs at this size.
- b, "The pooled shuffle, because 8,000 totals give the most worlds to count": pooling ignores that
  the same members were measured twice.
- d, "Wait another month, because a single pair of months cannot settle a change in price": the data
  already answers the question, and waiting buys nothing.

### Q4. In how many of 16 coin patterns does the gap reach Rs 400?

Four invented members' Q1 less Q2 differences are Rs 900, Rs 300, Rs 600 and minus Rs 200, a real
gap of Rs 400; a coin per member gives 16 patterns, and the count is of falls at least Rs 400.

The key is a, "2 of 16". The pattern as recorded gives Rs 400, and the pattern that also turns the
minus Rs 200 into plus Rs 200 gives Rs 500. Every other pattern turns one of the three falls into a
rise and lands below Rs 400.

- b, "1 of 16": misses the pattern that turns the one rise into a fall.
- c, "4 of 16": the count of a move that large either way, which the stem did not ask for.
- d, "8 of 16": half the patterns, which is where a gap of zero would sit.

### Q5. Which line about the Retail-Plus shares goes in the note to Meera?

Meera asked about Retail-Plus after the fall had been seen, and the flips give 0.029 counting falls
only and 0.057 counting either way.

The key is c, "Borderline: about 3 in 100 counting falls, 6 either way." Nobody chose a direction
before the fall was seen, so both counts go in the note, and together they read as borderline.

- a, "Real: 0.029 is under the 0.05 line, so the fall is proved.": calls a borderline share proof.
- b, "Noise: 0.057 is over the 0.05 line, so nothing happened.": reads a share at the edge as
  nothing having happened.
- d, "Real in one direction only, and noise in the other one.": a direction is decided before the
  test and reported, and picking it afterwards to suit the result is the error.

### Q6. Which share does the note report when pooling and flipping disagree on a heavy-buyer tier?

A design item. An invented tier of 30 members, where heavy buyers stay heavy, fell by Rs 314 a
member; pooling the 60 totals gives 0.24 counting falls and flipping each member's pair gives 0.004.

The key is b, "The flips' 0.004: pooling counts the gaps between members as chance". The same 30
members sit in both quarters and heavy buyers stay heavy, so most of the spread between totals is the
gap between members. Pooling counts that spread as chance, and the flips remove it.

- a, "The pooled 0.24, because it uses all 60 totals and not just 30 differences": more totals do
  not make a design that ignores the pairing fair.
- c, "The flips' 0.004, since the smaller share is always the one to report": the right number for
  the wrong reason, since a rule that always reports the smaller share would pick a pooled share
  whenever it happened to come out smaller.
- d, "Neither, until a third route can break the tie between the two numbers": the two routes answer
  different designs, and the design of this data picks one.

## Which wrong answer is worth arguing about?

Item 6, option a. Pooling uses more numbers, and some pairs will trust it for that. The numbers are
the same people counted twice: on this invented tier a member's Q1 and Q2 correlate at 0.95, so the
pooled deal mixes a Rs 314 fall with gaps of several thousand rupees between members and reads the
fall as ordinary. On Retail-Plus the correlation is 0.04, which is why pooling happened to land close
there, and a test chosen because it landed close on one file fails on the next tier where heavy
buyers stay heavy.

## Where does this show up at work?

Booking.com runs about 25,000 tests a year, and about nine in ten of its experiments improve
nothing, so its analysts read a share against the usual wobble week after week. A test that splits
users at random compares two groups of different people, a before-and-after on the same users is
paired, and the chance reference follows the design in both cases.
