## The idea being tested

A chance reference builds worlds in which nothing changed and counts how often those worlds make a
gap as large as the real one, and the design of the data decides how the worlds are built: the same
members measured twice keep their pairs and flip them, and two groups of different customers
shuffle their labels. The design items give new data and ask which reference fits, when the
textbook paired test takes over, and what pooling does on a tier where heavy buyers stay heavy. The
other items ask the question every share raises: in which world, and in which direction, was it
counted?

## The part worth arguing about

Item 6, option a. Pooling uses more numbers, and some pairs will trust it for that. The numbers
are the same people counted twice: on this invented tier a member's Q1 and Q2 correlate at about
0.9, so the pooled deal mixes a Rs 430 fall with gaps of several thousand rupees between members and
reads the fall as ordinary. On Retail-Plus the correlation is 0.04, which is why pooling happened to
land close there; the design, and never the luck of one file, decides the test.

## Where the pattern lives in production

Booking.com runs thousands of experiments a year, and its teams expect most ideas to fail, so an
analyst there reads a share against the usual wobble many times a week. Most of those tests compare
the same users before and after, or two randomly split groups, and the analyst's first question is
which of the two designs the data came from, because the chance reference follows it.
