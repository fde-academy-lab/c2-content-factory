# Solution: defensible, or wrong?

Answers: 1a 2b 3c 4b 5d 6c

---

| Item | Key | Why |
|---|---|---|
| **1** | `a` | It says what was computed and how finely the method can see. It reports a count rather than a probability, which is exactly right when the count is zero, and it does not claim a certainty five thousand shuffles cannot produce. |
| **2** | `b` | The p-value is a property of the chance-only worlds you built. This sentence makes it a property of your conclusion, which reverses what it measures. It is the single most-repeated error in business analytics and it is said with confidence every time. |
| **3** | `c` | A p-value answers "could chance have done this" and nothing else. A tiny p-value on a two percent difference is a precise measurement of something small, and on a large enough sample almost any difference reaches significance. |
| **4** | `b` | A large p-value means you have not shown a difference, which is not the same as showing there is none. Twelve orders cannot demonstrate flatness any more than they can demonstrate a rise. `d` is close and the problem is not the wording. |
| **5** | `d` | Three calls, and the shuffle answers only the first. Whether to act depends on the size of the effect and the cost of acting, neither of which the data has been asked about. `c` smuggles in the 0.05 threshold as if it settled the question. |
| **6** | `c` | This is the sentence a CEO keeps an analyst for. It refuses to guess and it names the thing that would end the refusal, which is what makes it useful rather than evasive. |
