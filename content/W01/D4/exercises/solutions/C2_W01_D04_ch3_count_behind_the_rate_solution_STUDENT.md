# Solution: chapter 3 set, the count behind 40 percent

Answers: 1b 2a 3d 4c 5c 6b

## The idea being tested

A rate is a numerator over a count, and the count decides how far the rate can be trusted. The count
that settles a rate is customers as well as orders, since more orders from the same few customers
add no new evidence. The design items give new segments and ask for the answer a budget request
deserves, the cheapest way to reach thirty customers, and which chance route fits which count. The
other items ask how far one order moves a rate, the interview's two rates, and a headline that
travels without its count.

## The part worth arguing about

Item 3, option a. Waiting for real customers sounds purer than paying for a test, and a paid test
does bring customers who may differ from the ones who arrive on their own. Here waiting costs about
eleven quarters of not knowing, against two weeks and Rs 3,750. The honest note proposes the test,
names the difference a paid customer might carry, and says what the test would have to show.

## Where the pattern lives in production

The Gates Foundation put large sums behind small schools after the best-scoring schools turned out
to be small; small schools also crowd the worst end of the table, because small counts swing both
ways. Any leaderboard of stores, cities or sales reps ranked by rate has the same shape, and the
question that exposes it is item 6's: what, and whom, does each rate stand on?
