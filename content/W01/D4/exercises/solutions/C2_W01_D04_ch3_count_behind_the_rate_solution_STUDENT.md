# Solution: chapter 3 set, the count behind 40 percent

Answers: 1d 2b 3c 4d 5a 6b

## The idea being tested

A rate is a numerator over a count, and the count decides how far the rate can be trusted. The
design items ask which option answers Meera, what would shorten the wait, how far one order moves a
rate, and when to switch between the two chance routes. The other items ask the same question of
the interview's rates and of a headline that travels without its count.

## The part worth arguing about

Item 1, option b. Waiting for thirty orders is the right action, and some pairs choose it for that
reason. It is the action the answer leads to, and it is incomplete as an answer: Meera asked today,
and the coin flips let the note tell her today how often chance makes the rise and what count would
reopen the question.

## Where the pattern lives in production

The Gates Foundation put large sums behind small schools after the best-scoring schools turned out
to be small; small schools also crowd the worst end of the table, because small counts swing both
ways. Any leaderboard of stores, cities or sales reps ranked by rate has the same shape, and the
question that exposes it is item 6's: what does each rate stand on?
