# Which answers hold in the chapter 3 set on the count behind Student's 40 percent, and why?

Answers: 1b 2a 3d 4c 5c 6b

Kalpa Retail's Student segment placed 40 percent more orders in Q2 than in Q1, the fastest rise of
its four segments, and Meera Raghavan, the CEO, asked whether acquisition budget should follow it.
Chapter 3 counted what the rise stands on, in orders and in customers, and found fewer than thirty
customers behind it. A coin flip per order, dealing each order to Q1 or Q2 at random 5,000 times,
makes a rise that large in 0.397 of those worlds. The rule of thumb the chapter drew: distrust a
rate with fewer than thirty customers behind it, since more orders from the same few customers add
no new evidence. Three of the six items are design items: 1, 3 and 5.

**Who needs the answer.** You, checking your six letters after the lab or tonight. A rate repeated
without its count is how a quarter's acquisition budget goes to a segment that may be flat next
quarter, and Meera hears that line on Monday unless you catch it here.

## Which idea does this set test?

A rate is a numerator over a count, and the count decides how far the rate can be trusted. The count
that settles a rate is customers as well as orders, since more orders from the same few customers add
no new evidence. The design items give new segments and ask for the answer a budget request deserves,
the cheapest way to reach thirty customers, and which chance route fits which count. The other items
ask how far one order moves a rate, the interview's two rates, and a headline that travels without
its count.

## Why is each key right, item by item?

### Q1. What goes back to the app team that wants Rs 2 lakh for a city whose orders rose 60 percent?

A design item. A new city's orders rose 60 percent, from 10 to 16, all from four customers; coin
flips on 26 orders make a rise that large in about 16 of every 100 worlds, and the app team asks for
a Rs 2 lakh launch budget.

The key is b, "Not yet: 16 in 100 is common, and four customers are too few". Chance makes the rise
about one time in six, and the orders come from four customers, so the rise is a lead: not yet, with
the number of customers that would reopen it.

- a, "Fund it: 60 percent is the fastest rise on the page": moves money on a rise a coin makes
  often.
- c, "No: 16 in 100 shows the rise is chance, so the city is flat": a common share says chance could
  have made the rise, and it cannot show that nothing rose.
- d, "Fund half of it, because chance makes the rise only 16 times in 100": reads one time in six as
  rare, and funds a rise chance makes often.

### Q2. How far does one order moved between quarters shift a 40 percent rise on 24 orders?

An invented segment's orders rose from 10 in Q1 to 14 in Q2, 40 percent; the item moves one Q2 order
into Q1.

The key is a, "About 18 percent". Thirteen against 11 is a rise of about 18 percent, so one order
out of 24 moved the headline by 22 points.

- b, "About 30 percent": takes the order out of Q2 and forgets to add it to Q1, 13 against 10.
- c, "About 36 percent": adds the order to Q1 and keeps the old gap of four orders, 4 over 11.
- d, "About 40 percent, unchanged": one order on 24 moves a rate a long way.

### Q3. Which plan goes to Meera for a rise that stands on 9 customers?

A design item. A segment's rise stands on 9 customers, new customers arrive at about 2 a quarter,
and a paid test aimed at new customers would bring about 25 in two weeks for Rs 3,750.

The key is d, "The paid test: Rs 3,750 reaches thirty customers in two weeks; waiting takes years".
Nine customers plus about 25 from the test passes thirty in two weeks. At 2 new customers a quarter,
waiting takes about eleven quarters.

- a, "Wait about eleven quarters for thirty customers, since paid customers are not real": sizes the
  wait correctly and then pays for it with years.
- b, "A paid test on the 9 customers already counted, to see if they buy again": more orders from the
  same 9 customers add orders and no new customers, so the count that settles the rate stays at 9.
- c, "Wait one quarter, since the next quarter's rise will settle it either way": one more quarter
  adds about 2 customers, which settles nothing.

### Q4. Which do you trust in an interview, 55 percent on 9 users or 38 percent on 2,000?

An interviewer sets 55 percent on 9 users against 38 percent on 2,000 and asks which you trust and
how you say it.

The key is c, "38 percent as the estimate, and 55 percent as a lead to measure". On 9 users one
person moves the rate by 11 points, so 55 percent sits inside what chance makes near a true 38. The
38 is the working estimate and the 55 a lead to measure on more users.

- a, "55 percent, since it is the higher of the two rates": the higher rate is not the truer one.
- b, "Neither, since two rates can only be read after a shuffle": the counts alone say enough to rank
  the two.
- d, "38 percent, since 2,000 users make it the true rate for everyone": 2,000 users make 38 stable,
  which is still short of true for everyone.

### Q5. Which chance route fits a segment on 18 orders, and which fits one on 60?

A design item. Kavya wants the exact chance share for two invented segments, one on 18 orders and one
on 60.

The key is c, "List every deal for the 18 orders, and flip coins for the 60". Eighteen orders can be
dealt in 262,144 ways, few enough to list; sixty can be dealt in over a billion billion ways, so the
flips are the only practical route there.

- a, "List every deal for both, since listing is exact": the list for 60 orders could never be
  finished.
- b, "Flip coins for both, since flips are always enough": the exact count is cheap on 18 orders and
  removes the sampling wobble.
- d, "List every deal for the 60 orders, and flip coins for the 18": swaps the two.

### Q6. Which line goes in the note about a 50 percent rise on 6 orders and a 30 percent rise on 300?

Two invented segments reach Meera's page: X rose from 6 orders to 9, 50 percent, and Y from 300 to
390, 30 percent.

The key is b, "Y's rise as worth testing on its count, and X's as a lead". Chance almost never makes
a 30 percent rise on 690 orders, so Y is worth testing on its count; three orders move X by 50
percent, so X is a lead.

- a, "X is the growth story, because its rise is the larger": the headline rate, repeated without its
  count.
- c, "Neither, since both rises could be the usual wobble": Y's count is large enough to read.
- d, "Both, weighted equally, as each is computed on its own orders": equal weight ignores the
  counts.

## Which wrong answer is worth arguing about?

Item 3, option a. Waiting for customers who arrive on their own sounds purer than paying for a test,
and a paid test does bring customers who may differ from the ones who come unasked. Here waiting
costs about eleven quarters of not knowing, against two weeks and Rs 3,750. The honest note proposes
the test, names the difference a paid customer might carry, and says what the test would have to
show.

## Where does this show up at work?

The Gates Foundation put money behind small schools after small schools turned up among the best
performers. The statistician Howard Wainer showed they were over-represented among the worst as
well, because small counts swing both ways. Any leaderboard of stores, cities or sales reps ranked by
a rate has the same shape, and the question that exposes it is item 6's: what, and whom, does each
rate stand on?
