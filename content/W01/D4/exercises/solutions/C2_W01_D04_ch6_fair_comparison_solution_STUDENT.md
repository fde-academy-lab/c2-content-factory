# Which answers hold in the chapter 6 set on what would settle the monsoon sale at Diwali, and why?

Marketing wants Kalpa Retail's monsoon sale, 15 percent off in August and aimed at Retail-Plus,
Kalpa's paid membership tier, again at Diwali and for more of the base. Chapter 4 had found that
inside each segment the customers who got the sale spent about 3 percent less. Chapter 6 asked who
got it, what else changed, and what would settle it. August against July shows Retail-Plus up 170
percent, but Retail-Core, the segment the sale was not aimed at, rose 73 percent the same month and
July stands on 4 orders. August's share of the quarter is 52.5 percent for Retail-Plus against 48.6
for Retail-Core, a gap label shuffles make in about 8 deals of 10 either way. Q1's months with no sale
show gaps of 4, 22 and 26 points. A coin that holds back one in five of the 70 Retail-Plus customers
on the sale's list at Diwali forgoes about Rs 4,200 at Marketing's own 6 percent. Three of the seven
items are design items: 2, 3 and 4.

## Which idea does this set test?

Every campaign readout needs three questions answered: who got it, who did not, and what else
changed. The design items ask for a hold-back sized under a limit the marketing lead sets, a second
route that shares nothing with the campaign's own list, and the comparison to settle for when a coin
is refused. The other items ask what to check first on a before-and-after jump, what two imperfect
routes can say together, how to hold the line when the split is called cherry-picking, and what each
comparison takes on trust.

## Why is each key right, item by item?

### Q1. Which check comes first on a 120 percent jump in a sale month?

An invented readout says a targeted segment delivered 120 percent more in its sale month than in the
month before, on a handful of orders each month.

The key is b, "Put an untargeted segment beside it over the same months, and count what each month
stands on". The untargeted segment shows what the month did on its own, and the count says how far a
handful of orders swings.

- a, "Rerun the before-and-after on revenue per order, so that the handful of orders stops
  mattering": per order still compares one month with another, on the same handful.
- c, "Accept the rise, since a jump of 120 percent is far too large for chance to make": on a handful
  of orders a month, swings of half or more happen with no sale at all.
- d, "Set the sale month against the same month last year, which takes the season out": last year's
  month differs in everything else too.

### Q2. Which hold-back keeps Marketing's cost under Rs 4,500 if its 6 percent lift is real?

A design item. The marketing lead accepts a Diwali hold-back that forgoes no more than Rs 4,500 if
Marketing's 6 percent lift is real; the sale's list holds 70 Retail-Plus customers, and those who did
not get the monsoon sale spent about Rs 5,000 each.

The key is a, "A random fifth, 14 customers, forgoing about Rs 4,200". A fifth of 70 is 14, and 14
times Rs 5,000 times 6 percent is Rs 4,200, inside the limit, with a coin deciding who is held back.
Fourteen customers are too few to see a lift as small as 6 percent, so the note says so, and the size
comes back when a later week teaches power, how many customers a test needs.

- b, "A random quarter, 18 customers, forgoing about Rs 5,400": 18 times Rs 300 is Rs 5,400, over the
  limit.
- c, "The 14 who spent least last quarter, forgoing under Rs 4,200": a rule chooses the lowest
  spenders, so they differ from the rest before the sale.
- d, "None; set Retail-Plus beside Retail-Core after Diwali instead": the change beside the change
  stands on a handful of orders a month, and it assumes the two segments move alike.

### Q3. Which second route shares nothing with the campaign's list, and what result would leave the campaign no claim?

A design item. An invented campaign month took 40 percent of its targeted segment's quarter and 33
percent of an untargeted segment's, a gap of 7 points, and the second route must share nothing with
the campaign's own list.

The key is b, "The same test on the two quarters before, when no campaign ran; gaps of 7 points or
more". Quarters with no campaign use other months of the same file and none of the campaign's list.
If they make gaps as large as 7 points, the campaign month carries no sign of the campaign.

- a, "The same gap under a new seed of shuffles; a much smaller count would settle it": a new seed
  reshuffles the same data and changes only the wobble.
- c, "The campaign month against the same month a year earlier; a smaller share then credits it": a
  year earlier differs in prices, customers and everything else.
- d, "The campaign's list split by segment; a lift inside both segments would confirm the campaign":
  the split stands on the campaign's own list, which the stem rules out.

### Q4. Which comparison fits a sale that runs in every northern store when the owners refuse a hold-back?

A design item. An invented chain runs a sale in every northern store, the franchise owners refuse any
hold-back, and the southern stores run no sale, with 24 months of revenue for both regions.

The key is b, "The north's change beside the south's over the months, saying both would have moved
alike". With every northern store in the sale and no coin allowed, the south over a long run of
months is the working comparison, and its one assumption is said aloud.

- a, "The north before and after the sale, since customers in the south differ from the north's":
  before and after credits the sale with the season.
- c, "Northern customers who bought in the sale against those who did not, in the same month": it
  compares who chose to buy, which the sale did not decide.
- d, "No comparison until the owners agree a hold-back, since only a coin can settle it": the sale
  runs this season either way, and waiting leaves it unmeasured.

### Q5. What do Finance's months and chapter 4's split let the note say together?

Finance's months show August taking about the same share of the quarter in both segments, and
chapter 4's split of the sale's list found about 3 percent less in both segments.

The key is c, "Neither finds a lift the sale can claim, and neither is fair". Two routes built on
different files agree that no lift can be claimed, and both rest on groups a rule chose.

- a, "The sale worked, since neither route could rule a lift out": failing to rule a lift out is a
  different thing from finding one.
- b, "The routes disagree, so the note waits for a third route to break the tie": "about the same"
  and "3 percent less" point the same way.
- d, "The split is right and the months wrong, since the split stands on more customers": size is a
  different question from fairness, and the split also rests on a chosen group.

### Q6. What do you say when the marketing lead calls the split cherry-picking?

The marketing lead replies, "Your split by segment is cherry-picking."

The key is d, "The segments were set before we looked, both fell alike, and a hold-back settles it".
Cherry-picking means choosing the cut after seeing the results; the segments were set before, they
agree, and the hold-back removes the argument.

- a, "Drop the split and send the blended figure, since a disputed number stays out of a CEO's note":
  drops the fairer comparison for the unfair one.
- b, "Split again by city and by payment method, and report whichever cut shows a lift": that is
  what cherry-picking is.
- c, "Agree, since any split can be chosen to show whatever the analyst wants": concedes a charge the
  method does not deserve.

### Q7. What does each of the four comparisons take on trust?

Meera asks what each comparison takes on trust: A before and after, B the change beside an
untargeted segment, C inside each segment and D a random hold-back, against 1 who got it inside a
segment was as good as random, 2 nothing beyond the coin, 3 nothing else changed between the months,
and 4 both segments would have moved alike.

The key is c, "A3, B4, C1, D2". Before and after trusts that the months changed nothing else (A3),
the change beside the change trusts parallel segments (B4), the split trusts that the choice inside
a segment was as good as random (C1), and the coin trusts nothing beyond itself (D2).

- a, "A3, B1, C4, D2": gives the change beside the change the split's assumption.
- b, "A4, B3, C1, D2": swaps the two month-based routes.
- d, "A1, B4, C3, D2": gives before and after the split's assumption.

## Which wrong answer is worth arguing about?

Item 4, option d. Holding out for a hold-back sounds like the rigorous answer, and chapter 6 did
argue that only a coin settles it. Here the owners have refused, the sale runs this season either
way, and the south has two years of months beside the north. The honest note runs the change beside
the change, says aloud that it trusts both regions to move alike, and asks for a hold-back at the
next sale that can still be designed.

## Where does this show up at work?

eBay stopped buying search ads on its own brand name in a controlled test and found that almost all
of the lost clicks arrived through ordinary search results instead; the researchers reported that
those ads had no measurable short-term benefit, and that frequent buyers, who would have bought
anyway, took most of the ad spend. Any campaign aimed at customers who already buy has that shape,
and a random hold-back is how a team finds out before it spends.
