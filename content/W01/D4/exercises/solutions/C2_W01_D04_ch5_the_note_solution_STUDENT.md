# Which answers hold in the chapter 5 set on what goes on Meera's one page, and why?

Answers: 1b 2a 3c 4d 5c 6d 7b

Meera Raghavan, Kalpa Retail's CEO, wants three answers on one page she can read in two minutes, and
"we do not know yet" is allowed if it says what would tell her. Chapter 5 wrote the morning's answers
as a four-part note, claim, evidence, caveat and action, in 193 words, and audited it line by line:
the Retail-Plus fall is borderline and small against the company, Rs 24,420 a quarter or 0.19
percent; Student's 40 percent stands on fewer than thirty customers, so not yet; and the monsoon sale
did not work as designed, since inside each segment its customers spent 3 percent less. The audit
asks of each line whether its number carries its base, its count or its chance, and a caveat, and
the headline note failed it in all nine cells. Three of the seven items are design items: 1, 4 and 5.

**Who needs the answer.** You, checking your seven letters after the lab or tonight. The note is the
one thing Meera reads, and a line that loses its base, or a note whose decision does not follow from
its own numbers, sends money the wrong way with Marketing in the room.

**The questions on the way.**

- Which idea does this set test?
- Why is each key right, item by item?
- Which wrong answer is worth arguing about?
- Where does this show up at work?

## Which idea does this set test?

A CEO's note carries each number with its partner, a share with its world and its direction, a rupee
figure with its whole, a rate with its count and a lift with its groups, and puts a decision in the
same place. The design items give new situations and ask which form a weekly request needs, what to
cut when a note runs long, and which second route catches a note whose decision does not follow from
its numbers. The other items ask whether a line passes the audit, how "not yet" is said so that it
still answers, and the order of the audit.

## Why is each key right, item by item?

### Q1. Which form answers the CFO's same three questions every Monday for a quarter?

A design item. The CFO wants the same three answers every Monday for a quarter, each on the same
bases.

The key is b, "A small dashboard with fixed bases, and a note on what moved". The same metrics on
the same bases every week are what a small dashboard does well, and the note shrinks to what moved
and what it means.

- a, "A fresh four-part note every week, rewritten from the numbers up": rewriting the whole note
  weekly repeats the bases for nothing.
- c, "A yes or no per question, since the bases never change": drops every caveat, and a weekly yes
  or no hides what moved.
- d, "A slide deck each Monday, one slide for each question": needs a meeting each Monday, and the
  reasoning lives in the talk.

### Q2. What must be added to "Student is up 40%" before it passes the audit?

The headline note's Student line reads "Student is up 40%."

The key is a, "Its count in customers, how often chance makes it, and what reopens it". A rate
travels with its count, counted in customers, and with its chance, and "not yet" names what would
reopen it.

- b, "Its count in orders, how often chance makes it, and what reopens it": more orders from the
  same few customers add no new evidence, so a count in orders makes a thin rise look solid.
- c, "The rise in rupees, so that Meera can set it beside the Retail-Plus fall": rupees size a rise
  and leave its chance and its count unanswered.
- d, "How often chance makes it, since a share says more than any count": the share leaves out the
  customers, the count the rule of thumb reads.

### Q3. Which version of the discount line passes the audit?

The marketing lead will read the discount line on Monday, and four versions are on the table.

The key is c, "Not as designed: inside each segment its customers spent 3 percent less; hold back a
random slice at Diwali." It has the claim, the evidence inside each segment and an action that makes
the next comparison fair.

- a, "The monsoon sale lifted revenue 6 percent across everyone it reached, so repeat it for Diwali
  at the same 15 percent.": the blend, credited to the sale.
- b, "The monsoon sale lowered spend in both segments, so Marketing should run no sale of any kind
  at Diwali this year.": overclaims the other way and offers no way to learn.
- d, "The monsoon sale's effect is unclear from the data we have, so we will need more analysis
  before anyone decides.": a caveat with no evidence and no action.

### Q4. Which cut brings a 240-word note under Meera's 200 words and keeps it honest?

A design item. Three lines run 90, 70 and 80 words, 240 in all, against a ceiling of 200.

The key is d, "Cut each line's evidence to one number and its base; keep each caveat". Forty words
must go, and the evidence can lose its second and third figures without losing its base; the caveat
is the part a CEO keeps an analyst for.

- a, "Drop the caveat from each of the three lines, which saves about 40 words": saves the words by
  cutting the part that makes the note honest.
- b, "Move each caveat to a footnote below the note, which saves the 40 words": a caveat in a
  footnote is one Meera does not read in two minutes.
- c, "Drop the Student line, since all it says is not yet": "not yet" with its threshold is an answer
  she asked to hear.

### Q5. Which check catches the colleague's campaign note before Meera reads it?

A design item. A colleague's note on an invented campaign says "repeat it" while its own split shows
every segment fell, and every figure in it traces to a computed number.

The key is c, "Applying the day's rules to its numbers and comparing decisions". The note's action
does not follow from its own split, which only a check of decisions against the day's rules can
catch.

- a, "Tracing every figure back to the chapter number that made it": every figure traces, so tracing
  passes the note.
- b, "Reading the note aloud to someone from outside the analytics team": reading aloud finds tone and
  misses a wrong decision.
- d, "Counting the words against the 200-word ceiling": a short wrong note is still wrong.

### Q6. What do you say when Meera asks for a yes or no on Student?

Meera replies, "Just give me yes or no on Student."

The key is d, "Not yet: we watch Student until more customers buy, thirty or more." It is a claim, a
caveat and an action in one sentence, and it names what turns it into a yes.

- a, "Yes: it is our fastest-growing segment, and the rise is real arithmetic.": the headline
  reading, with the count left out.
- b, "No: a segment this small is never worth any budget at all.": overclaims from size, which nobody
  measured.
- c, "It depends, and I need another week of analysis first.": a delay with no threshold is the answer
  Meera asked the team not to give.

### Q7. In which order do the audit's four steps run on one line of the note?

Kavya hands over the line "Retail-Plus revenue fell 34%" to audit before it goes to Meera, and the
four steps are to find the number, name its base, attach its count or chance, and state the caveat.

The key is b, "Find the number, name its base, attach its count or chance, state the caveat". The
audit starts from the figure as written, then asks what it is a share of, how many stand behind it
or how often chance makes it, and what would change it. The base comes before the count, because a
count without its base is still unreadable.

- a, "State the caveat, find the number, name its base, attach its count or chance": a caveat written
  before the number is known has nothing to qualify.
- c, "Name the base, state the caveat, find the number, attach its count or chance": the base cannot
  be named before the number is found.
- d, "Attach the count or chance, find the number, name its base, state the caveat": a chance
  reference means nothing until the number and its base are fixed.

## Which wrong answer is worth arguing about?

Item 5, option a. Tracing every figure is the check most teams run, and some pairs will pick it by
reflex. It passes this note, because the note's numbers are right and only its decision is wrong. A
note can quote a correct split and still recommend the opposite of what the split says, and the check
that catches it applies the rule the analysis ended on and compares the decision.

## Where does this show up at work?

Amazon's meetings run on six-page narrative memos read in silence at the start, and Jeff Bezos's
2017 letter to shareholders says the company does not use slide presentations. The discipline holds
at any length: every claim carries its evidence and its caveat, and the reader leaves with a decision
and the reasoning behind it.
