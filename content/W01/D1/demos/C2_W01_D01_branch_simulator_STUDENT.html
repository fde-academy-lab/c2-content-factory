Experiment A · chapter 1 · Kalpa totals

What happens to sales when the cancelled orders come out?

Situation. The first total anyone computes on the 30 orders is Rs 5,44,810, and it is about to go into Meera's note as sales. Four of the 30 orders were cancelled.

Hypothesis. Taking the cancelled orders out lowers sales by their Rs 9,050 and removes four orders, all from one channel.

Watch for. The rose bar in the bridge and the order count under the new total.

The bridge appears here after the run.

What happened.

Why it matters. A cancelled order never became a sale. All four are store orders, so store's order count is overstated by 4 in 10, and the growth baseline includes demand that never arrived.

Name the definition before the number: booked, "not cancelled", or delivered.

Experiment B · chapter 3 · Kalpa totals

How many customers are there once each id counts once?

Situation. The extract has 30 rows, and a hurried count reports 30 customers, so orders per customer reads 30 / 30 = 1.00 and marketing says nobody comes back.

Hypothesis. Counting each customer id once gives fewer customers than rows, and some customers turn out to have bought more than once.

Watch for. The customers bar falling and a second bar, the customers who came back, rising from zero.

The count appears here after the run.

What happened.

Why it matters. At 1.00 orders per customer, acquisition looks like the only branch and the Rs 12 crore looks justified. At 1.30, with 7 customers back, frequency is a live branch that costs far less to test.

Count customers by their id, never by the rows.

Experiment C · chapter 4 · invented orders

How far can one large order move the mean and the median?

Situation. Five invented orders sit between Rs 1,900 and Rs 2,600. An invented order of Rs 90,000 then joins them as a sixth, which is the shape Anand warned about.

Hypothesis. The mean jumps to several times its old value, and the median barely moves.

Watch for. The rose mean line leaving the pile of dots, and whether the green median line moves at all.

The strip appears here after the run.

What happened.

Why it matters. On Kalpa's 30 orders the mean is Rs 18,160 and the median Rs 2,205, and only 1 of 30 orders sits above the mean. Marketing valuing a first order at the mean would expect about eight times what a typical order brings.

Report the median when one order can move the mean, and say why.

Experiment D · chapter 5 · Kalpa totals

Do two 10 percent lifts make 20 percent?

Situation. Marketing's plan lifts customers by 10 percent and a loyalty scheme lifts orders per customer by 10 percent. The slide says revenue in the three consumer segments, Rs 64,810, grows 20 percent, to Rs 77,772.

Hypothesis. Through the tree the lifts multiply, so revenue grows 21 percent and lands higher than the slide says.

Watch for. The small green bar after the two lifts, which is the lift on the lift.

The bridge appears here after the run.

What happened.

Why it matters. Rs 648 is small on one quarter of consumer revenue, and the same habit of adding percentages hides a fall where one branch rises and another drops, which is where a plan goes wrong.

Lifts multiply along the tree: two 10 percent lifts make 21 percent.

Experiment E · chapter 5 · Kalpa totals

Can a discount sell more and still earn less?

Situation. A 15 percent discount is proposed, and marketing expects it to lift quantity by 10 percent. The pitch says more orders means more revenue.

Hypothesis. Price falls by 15 percent and quantity rises by 10, so revenue ends at 0.85 times 1.10 of today, which is a fall.

Watch for. Where the bridge lands against the starting bar.

The bridge appears here after the run.

What happened.

Why it matters. A discount trades margin for quantity, and it pays only when quantity rises by more than the price falls; at 15 percent off, quantity has to rise by about 17.6 percent just to stand still.

Recompute revenue through the tree, never by adding the percentages.

Does store's 91.6 percent of revenue hold in the three consumer segments?

Situation. By channel, store carries 91.6 percent of booked revenue on 10 of the 30 orders, and a draft says the growth plan should be store-led.

Hypothesis. Meera's plan concerns the three consumer segments, and once the view keeps only those segments the three channels sit much closer together than the headline says.

Watch for. Store's dark bar against its light one, and which channel leads once the view keeps the consumer segments.

The shares appear here after the run.

What happened.

Why it matters. A store-led plan would invest behind revenue from outside the segments the plan concerns. Inside them the leaks matter more: Rs 14,970 of web's Rs 27,290 came back, and Rs 9,050 of store's Rs 18,920 was cancelled, while app delivered all of its Rs 18,600.

Count the orders behind a share before the share decides anything.

07 · Glossary

Which words does the day use, and where did each first matter?

Revenue tree
The tree draws revenue as customers times orders per customer times average order value, and average order value splits into items per order times price per item, less discounts.The thinking drawn in the story, before chapter 1.
Branch
A branch is one box of the tree, a metric the business can move at its own cost: acquisition costs marketing, frequency costs retention, and basket costs merchandising.The story; chapter 5.
Numerator and denominator
They are the top and the bottom of every rate, and both are said out loud before the rate is computed.Chapter 2.
Booked
Booked counts every order placed in the window, whatever happened to it afterwards.Chapter 1.
Not cancelled
Not cancelled keeps the booked orders less the ones cancelled before they shipped.Chapter 1, the fix.
Delivered
Delivered keeps the orders that reached the customer and stayed there, so returns and cancellations are both out.Chapter 1, the fix; the escalated case.
Distinct customer
A distinct customer is an id counted once however many rows it appears on, and in Python the count is the length of a set of ids.Chapter 3, the trap and its fix.
Orders per customer
Orders per customer divides orders by distinct customers, and it is the frequency branch of the tree.Chapter 3.
Repeat customer
A repeat customer placed more than one order in the window.Chapter 3, the build.
Mean
The mean spreads the total evenly over the count; it multiplies back to the total, and one large order can drag it.Chapter 4.
Median
The median is the middle of the sorted list, or the average of the two middles when the count is even, and one large order cannot drag it.Chapter 4, the fix.
Compounding
Lifts on the branches multiply, so each lift also applies to the other's gain.Chapter 5.
Window
A window is the dates a number covers; one window shows the shape of revenue, and two windows show which branch moved.Chapter 6, the window's edge; Tuesday's question.
Usual gap
The usual gap is the median number of days returning customers took between a first and a second order, 45 in the day's file, so a one-time buyer who ordered inside the last 45 days is too recent to call lost.Chapter 6, the trap and its fix.
Channel share
A channel's share is its revenue over all revenue, read only after counting the orders behind it.The second case.
Consumer view
The consumer view keeps the orders whose segment is Retail-Core, Retail-Plus or Student, the three consumer segments Meera's growth plan concerns, and your own code counts how many orders it keeps.Chapter 5; the second case.
